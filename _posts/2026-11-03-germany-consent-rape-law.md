On 2 November 2026 Germany's justice minister, Stefanie Hubig, published a draft law that would make it a crime to sexually harass someone with words or gestures, raise minimum sentences for group rape, and give prosecutors 20 years to bring any rape case to court ([tagesschau](https://www.tagesschau.de/inland/hubig-sexualstrafrecht-102.html)). Women's groups welcomed much of it. But the most discussed part of the draft is what it leaves out: the definition of rape itself stays as it is. There is no "only yes means yes" rule, at least for now, even though Hubig had recently said she supported one. According to [LTO](https://www.lto.de/recht/nachrichten/n/catcalling-wird-strafbar-aber-kein-nur-ja-heisst-ja-vergewaltigung), the draft contains only what the governing coalition already agrees on, and the ministry says it is still working on a concrete proposal for consent.

That sounds like a quarrel over a slogan. It is not. The wording of a rape law decides which cases can reach a court at all, and it matters most for the women whose cases look least like the stereotype.

## What the German draft actually does

The draft has three main parts, according to [LTO](https://www.lto.de/recht/nachrichten/n/catcalling-wird-strafbar-aber-kein-nur-ja-heisst-ja-vergewaltigung) and [beck-aktuell](https://www.beck-aktuell.de/heute-im-recht/rechtspolitik-gesetzgebung/reform-sexualstrafrecht-hubig-referentenentwurf-2026-11-02).

**Harassment without touching.** Today German law punishes sexual harassment only when there is physical contact. The draft adds a new offence for sexual remarks or gestures that are "suitable to intimidate or pressure" the victim, with up to a year in prison or a fine. The ministry pointed to a 2017 Federal Court of Justice decision that a 65-year-old man's sexual remark to an 11-year-old girl was not a crime.

**A 20-year time limit.** Because of how the 2016 law was drafted, some rapes, including rapes of unconscious or drugged victims, currently expire after only five years. In one case cited by the ministry, 65 of 67 alleged assaults by a man on his incapacitated wife had already expired before the investigation began ([beck-aktuell](https://www.beck-aktuell.de/heute-im-recht/rechtspolitik-gesetzgebung/reform-sexualstrafrecht-hubig-referentenentwurf-2026-11-02)). The draft sets 20 years for all rapes. Since the clock only starts when the victim turns 30, a rape could then be prosecuted at least until she is 50.

**Harsher minimums for group offences.** Group rape would carry at least five years, and other group sexual assaults at least three instead of two.

The federal states and civil-society groups can comment until 30 November, after which the cabinet must decide. The women's rights group Terre des Femmes welcomed the longer time limit, which matters most for drugged victims who learn what happened only when videos surface, but said the consent rule is "above all" what is missing ([MDR](https://www.mdr.de/nachrichten/deutschland/politik/sexualstrafrecht-reform-kritik-hubig,gruppenvergewaltigung-100.html)).

## Three ways to define rape

Around the world, rape laws follow roughly three models.

**The force model.** Rape requires violence, a threat, or a victim who could not resist. Until recently this was the norm in much of Europe. In 2021 Equality Now found that 23 of 43 jurisdictions in the Americas still required force, threat or the victim's helplessness ([Equality Now](https://equalitynow.org/resource/factsheets/consent-based-rape-definitions/)).

**"No means no".** Since 2016 German law punishes sexual acts against a person's "recognisable will". The victim does not have to fight, but her refusal must be visible, in words or in behaviour. Austria uses a similar approach ([DW](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-fails-to-agree-on-legal-definition-of-rape/a-68195256)).

**"Only yes means yes".** Sex is lawful only when both people show, in words or in actions, that they want it. Sweden (2018) and Spain (2022) are the best-known examples.

The gap between the second and third models is the woman who freezes. Many victims neither fight nor say no; they go still. Under "no means no" there may be nothing "recognisable" for a court to point to. A conservative CDU lawmaker, Axel Müller, put it plainly to LTO: in cases of freezing "there is a gap in criminal liability", because the victim sends no signal that makes her refusal recognisable ([LTO](https://www.lto.de/recht/nachrichten/n/catcalling-wird-strafbar-aber-kein-nur-ja-heisst-ja-vergewaltigung)). The upper house of parliament, the Bundesrat, had asked for a change this summer for exactly this reason.

Counting is tricky because organisations draw the lines differently. Amnesty International counts Germany among countries with consent-based laws and says 21 European countries now have one, up from 8 in 2018, with France and Norway among the latest ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/04/consent-based-definition-of-rape/)). The European Women's Lobby, by contrast, counted only 14 EU states using "only yes means yes" in 2023 and put Germany in the "no means no" group ([DW](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-fails-to-agree-on-legal-definition-of-rape/a-68195256)).

## Does changing the words change anything?

**Sweden: yes, measurably.** Sweden's crime-prevention council, Brå, found that rape convictions rose from 190 in 2017 to 333 in 2019, a 75% increase after the law changed ([Brå 2020](https://bra.se/english/publications/archive/2020-07-01-the-new-consent-law-in-practice)). In its 2025 follow-up, prosecutions had roughly doubled from 236 in 2017 to 455 in 2020, and convictions in "new" cases, those that could not have been prosecuted before, reached 121 in 2023 ([Brå 2025](https://bra.se/english/publications/archive/2025-04-08-application-and-consequences-of-the-consent-law)). But Brå also found that half of the new cases it identified in 2019 still ended in acquittal, and that the new crime of "negligent rape" raises hard questions of legal certainty.

**Spain: a warning about drafting.** Spain's 2022 law merged the old offences of sexual abuse and sexual assault into one, with wider sentence ranges. Because Spanish law applies a milder law retroactively, courts cut at least 1,233 sentences and released at least 126 offenders by November 2023, according to the judiciary's own figures ([La Vanguardia](https://www.lavanguardia.com/vida/20231124/9404411/1-233-reducciones-pena-126-excarcelado-ley-solo.html)). Parliament raised the penalties again in April 2023 ([BOE](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2023-10213)).

**England and Wales: a good definition is not enough.** The UK is on Amnesty's list of countries with consent-based laws ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2025/10/france-historic-victory-as-french-law-adopts-consent-based-definition-of-rape/)), yet only 2.6% of rapes recorded in the year to March 2024 had led to a charge when first published, rising to 4.6% as investigations were completed. Almost half of recorded rapes are closed for "evidential difficulties" ([Home Office](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/crime-outcomes-in-england-and-wales-2024-to-2025/crime-outcomes-in-england-and-wales-2024-to-2025)).

**South Africa: the definition can still contain a loophole.** South African law defines rape as sex without consent, but the accused can be acquitted if he believed, even unreasonably, that she agreed. A survivor's case reached the Constitutional Court in September 2025; advocates cited 591 convictions out of 42,780 reported rapes in 2022–23, about 1.4%. Not every women's organisation agreed: the Women's Legal Centre argued that the law need not change and that trial courts should simply test whether any claimed belief in consent had reasonable grounds. The court reserved judgment ([Daily Maverick](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2025-09-25-concourt-asked-to-redefine-consent-in-sexual-offences-cases/)).

## Why governments hesitate

The arguments against "only yes means yes" deserve a serious hearing. When the EU tried to write a consent-based definition into its 2024 directive on violence against women, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Hungary, Poland and others blocked it. Germany's then justice minister said the EU lacked the legal power to harmonise this part of criminal law ([DW](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-fails-to-agree-on-legal-definition-of-rape/a-68195256)). France's justice minister went further and asked whether criminal law should define "the consent of a victim instead of focusing on the responsibility of the criminal", warning that the burden of proof could shift onto the victim and that sex could be "contractualised" ([Euractiv](https://www.euractiv.com/section/justice-home-affairs/news/minister-of-justice-clarifies-frances-position-over-rape-inclusion-in-eu-directive/)). France later changed its own law in 2025 anyway ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2025/10/france-historic-victory-as-french-law-adopts-consent-based-definition-of-rape/)).

Practitioners raise sharper, practical objections. At a 2025 debate in Vienna, where Austria's justice minister wants a similar rule, the head of the country's defence lawyers' association asked: "Is a nod enough, do I need a video, do I have to keep it?", and warned that the accused might in effect have to prove his innocence. A senior prosecutor said she saw no gap in the current law, and that the real problem, one person's word against another's, would not change. A criminal-law professor replied that nobody would have to say "yes" out loud, that non-verbal agreement counts, and that the change would clarify rather than tighten the law. She gave the example of a woman who let it happen only because she did not want a scene that would wake the children: she said neither no nor yes ([Die Presse](https://www.diepresse.com/20204760/nur-ja-heisst-ja-warum-anwaelte-und-anklaeger-anna-sporrers-plan-sehen)).

Those concerns are real, but none of them changes the basic rule of a fair trial: the prosecution must still prove the absence of consent beyond reasonable doubt. What changes is the question in court, from "did she resist or refuse?" to "what made him believe she wanted it?".

Beyond Europe, the debate starts further back. None of the 22 Arab League states explicitly criminalises rape within marriage, according to Equality Now, and some exclude it in writing ([Equality Now](https://equalitynow.org/news/news-and-insights/marital-rape-a-hidden-crime-behind-closed-doors/)).

## Opinion: Germany fixed the clock but not the gap

The German draft is good law. Punishing intimidating sexual remarks tells girls that the street is theirs too.

But Germany has repaired the time limit and left the main gap open. A woman who freezes is not giving a recognisable signal of anything; she is in shock. The Swedish evidence shows that a consent rule brings real cases into court that were out of reach before, while also showing that courts still acquit when the evidence is thin, as they should. The open problems there are about legal certainty at the edges, not about innocent men filling prisons. The Spanish experience shows that the danger lies in careless sentencing rules, not in the principle. The Austrian prosecutor is right that no wording fixes word-against-word cases; that is an argument for better evidence work, such as quick forensic examinations, not against naming the crime correctly. The English and South African numbers are a reminder that no wording will fix police work, evidence and myths about how "real" victims behave.

The honest position is that the definition is necessary but not sufficient. Germany has until 30 November to hear that argument from its own states and women's groups. It should listen.
