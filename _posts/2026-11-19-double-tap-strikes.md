On the morning of 18 November, Russian strikes hit the UNESCO-listed historic centre of Odesa three times, killing four people and wounding 13, according to the regional governor. Ukraine's president said there were ["double-tap strikes after first responders and police officers had already arrived at the scene"](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/ukraine-says-russian-strikes-kill-four-in-odesa). Early reports do not say any rescuer died this time. But the phrase "double tap" has become one of the most feared in modern war, and not only in Ukraine.

The idea is simple. You strike a place. People rush in: firefighters, paramedics, neighbours, journalists. Then you strike the same place again. This explainer looks at where the tactic has been documented, what international law actually says, how the armies accused of it explain themselves, and why it is so rarely punished.

## A tactic with many users

It is tempting to treat the double tap as one country's signature. The record says otherwise.

**Pakistan, 2009–2012.** A three-month investigation by the Bureau of Investigative Journalism found evidence that [at least 50 civilians were killed in follow-up CIA drone strikes](https://www.thebureauinvestigates.com/stories/2012-02-04/cia-tactics-in-pakistan-include-targeting-rescuers-and-funerals/) when they went to help victims, and more than 20 in strikes on funerals and mourners. The US government did not publish casualty figures for these strikes.

**Yemen, 2016.** On 8 October 2016 the Saudi-led coalition bombed a packed funeral hall in Sanaa. UN sanctions monitors told the Security Council that a second strike followed the first within minutes and hit the wounded and those helping them, and that this ["double tap" violated international humanitarian law](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-yemen-security-saudi-un-idUSKCN12K2F1/). The coalition's own investigation blamed [wrong information from a Yemeni allied party](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-yemen-security-saudi/saudi-coalition-attack-on-yemen-funeral-based-on-bad-information-investigator-idUSKBN12F090/) and recommended compensation and accountability.

**Syria.** The White Helmets rescue service has published a report identifying [122 potential double-tap incidents](https://reliefweb.int/attachments/b3337f37-9955-42c6-849d-6a9f83f816a4/People%20need%20me%20-%20Report%20on%20double-taps%20against%20the%20White%20Helmets%20EN.pdf) against its volunteers, attributing about a quarter to Russian forces and most of the rest to the forces of the former Assad government. These are the findings of a party to the conflict's rescue service, not of a court.

**Gaza, 2025.** On 25 August 2025 an Israeli strike hit a stairwell at Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis; about ten minutes later [a second strike hit journalists and rescuers who had gathered there](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cd6n97gj1nqo). At least 20 people died, including five journalists and four health workers. The Israeli military said it had targeted [a camera it believed Hamas was using](https://apnews.com/article/israel-palestinians-hamas-gaza-war-08-26-2025-0f1be1b4e3231e0cbec54ce837cc6af9) to watch its troops and opened an inquiry.

**Ukraine, since 2022.** The Ukrainian human-rights group Truth Hounds counted [401 incidents up to October 2025](https://truth-hounds.org/en/cases/calculated-harm/) in which Russian attacks killed or injured emergency responders, damaged fire stations or vehicles, or forced crews to stop work, with at least 42 responders killed. Ukraine's State Emergency Service has reported [more than 100 of its rescuers killed](https://www.ukrinform.net/rubric-ato/4031032-107-ukrainian-rescuers-killed-since-start-of-fullscale-war.html) since the full-scale invasion, from all causes. The deadliest single case came in Odesa on 15 March 2024, when a second missile hit the same area as rescuers worked and [at least 20 people were killed](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/03/15/europe/missile-strike-odesa-russia-ukraine-double-tap-intl). Russia's Defence Ministry described its strikes in the Odesa region that weekend as hitting [sites used to assemble and test Ukrainian uncrewed boats](https://rg.ru/2024/03/17/udarom-vs-rf-porazheny-mesta-sborki-bezekipazhnyh-katerov-vsu-v-odesskoj-oblasti.html). Russian officials have, in turn, accused Ukrainian drones of hitting emergency vehicles inside Russian-held areas; I could not find independent verification of those specific claims.

**The Caribbean, 2025.** Even outside a declared war, the question came up. In a US strike on an alleged drug-smuggling boat on 2 September 2025, a commander ordered a second strike some 41 minutes later that killed two survivors clinging to the wreck; he reportedly told Congress it was meant to destroy the remaining part of the vessel and its cargo. Legal scholars writing in [Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-troubling-defense-of-the-second-strike) called that defence troubling.

## What the law actually says

Here is the uncomfortable part: **international law does not ban hitting the same place twice.** There is no "double-tap rule". The legality depends on who and what the second strike is aimed at, and what the attacker knew.

Three principles do the work:

- **Distinction.** Only military objectives may be attacked. Civilians, including civilian rescuers, may not.
- **Special protection.** Medical personnel, and civil-defence workers doing rescue, [must be "respected and protected in all circumstances"](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule25) as long as they do not take part in hostilities. The ICRC lists [rescue among the protected civil-defence tasks](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/civil-defence). Wounded fighters who can no longer fight are ["hors de combat"](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v2/rule47) and may not be finished off.
- **Proportionality and precautions.** Even against a lawful target, an attack is forbidden if the expected civilian harm is excessive compared with the military advantage, and commanders must take feasible precautions to avoid it.

So a second strike *can* be lawful: for example, if the first missed a weapons depot, or if enemy fighters return to recover a launcher. A [BYU Law Review analysis](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1496&context=byuplr) argues that a pre-planned follow-up strike that does not reassess who is now on the ground, when rescuers are entirely foreseeable, is likely to breach distinction and proportionality and can amount to a war crime. When the timing seems designed to catch responders, as Ukrainian officials and the [Kyiv Independent](https://kyivindependent.com/double-tap-attack-understanding-one-of-russias-cruelest-tactics-in-ukraine/) describe in Russian attacks, the strongest charge becomes possible: deliberately targeting protected people.

## How attackers explain themselves

Taking those explanations seriously is part of understanding the tactic, even when the explanation does not hold up.

1. **"The target was military."** Russia says it hits drone workshops and military sites; Israel said the Nasser strike was aimed at a Hamas camera. The argument is that the dead are incidental. The weakness is the second strike itself: by the time it lands, the people present are, in most documented cases, the wounded and those helping them. In the Nasser case, NBC News later quoted an Israeli soldier who said the military [could find no evidence linking the camera to Hamas](https://www.nbcnews.com/world/gaza/year-israeli-strikes-killed-journalists-hospital-no-one-held-accountab-rcna594406). That is one anonymous account, not a finding.
2. **"The responders were not really civilians."** In Pakistan, the Bureau's own investigation noted that [the Taliban often sealed off strike sites and retrieved the dead](https://www.thebureauinvestigates.com/stories/2012-02-04/cia-tactics-in-pakistan-include-targeting-rescuers-and-funerals/), so some of those arriving were fighters, and US officials insisted the strikes were legal. Armies also note that armed groups sometimes use ambulances or civilian clothes. Where that happens, protection can be lost. But the burden is on the attacker to know, not to assume, and a whole category of people (everyone who runs toward an explosion) cannot be presumed hostile.
3. **"It was a mistake."** The Saudi-led coalition said bad intelligence and a breach of procedure caused the Sanaa strike. Mistakes are real in war. A pattern of the same "mistake", repeated over years, is harder to explain that way.

## The hidden cost: slower rescues

The double tap does damage beyond those it kills. Once rescuers expect a second strike, they have to wait, take cover, or work in shifts while monitoring the sky. Ukrainian crews describe [pausing operations when drones are detected](https://kyivindependent.com/double-tap-attack-understanding-one-of-russias-cruelest-tactics-in-ukraine/), while stressing that they cannot wait for long when people are trapped and bleeding. Every minute of hesitation can cost a life under the rubble. That is why many lawyers see the tactic as an attack on the whole idea of rescue, not only on the rescuers.

## Why almost no one is punished

Investigations exist; verdicts mostly do not. Of the cases above, I found no criminal conviction of anyone for a double-tap strike. A year after the Nasser Hospital strike, [no one had been held accountable](https://www.nbcnews.com/world/gaza/year-israeli-strikes-killed-journalists-hospital-no-one-held-accountab-rcna594406). Russia, the United States, Israel and Saudi Arabia are all outside the [International Criminal Court's membership](https://asp.icc-cpi.int/states-parties). The Saudi-led coalition investigated itself. States tend to investigate their own forces, slowly, and to treat outside findings as hostile propaganda, which is precisely what the other side says about their findings.

## Opinion: The second strike should carry the burden of proof

The law is right not to ban every second strike. Wars are messy, and a blanket rule would be ignored. But the law could reasonably shift the burden: when a second strike lands on a scene where rescuers are foreseeable, the attacking state should have to show what military target remained, what it knew about who was there, and why it could not wait. Today, the families of the dead have to prove intent against governments that keep their targeting secret.

This matters most for the people the tactic is built to deter: the firefighter, the nurse, the neighbour who runs toward the smoke. Every society, whatever side it is on, depends on such people. A rule that protects them should not depend on which capital the missile came from. Applied honestly, it would apply to Moscow, Washington, Riyadh and Jerusalem alike, and to anyone else who learns that the cruellest moment to strike is the moment someone comes to help.
