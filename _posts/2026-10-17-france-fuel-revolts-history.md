## What happened on 17 October

On Saturday, people marched in cities across France against high prices. The call came from former yellow vests and from young people, under the banner "yellow and youth", and it merged with four weeks of student protests. [AFP reported](https://www.afp.com/en/french-protesters-clash-police-during-nationwide-demonstrations) that more than 600 demonstrations were planned and that tens of thousands marched in Paris alone. The interior ministry [counted 272 arrests nationwide](https://www.france24.com/fr/info-en-continu/20261017-journ%C3%A9e-de-mobilisation-contre-la-vie-ch%C3%A8re-les-derniers-d%C3%A9veloppements) by 3 pm.

The day also turned violent in several cities. In Marseille, several dozen people [forced the doors of the town hall](https://www.france24.com/fr/info-en-continu/20261017-a-marseille-des-manifestants-ont-p%C3%A9n%C3%A9tr%C3%A9-bri%C3%A8vement-dans-le-hall-de-la-mairie), smashed furniture and knocked over a bust of Marianne, shouting "it's the Revolution, we're taking the Bastille". A tourism office was set on fire in Lyon, and the [BBC reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/live/ckpdg1v41562t) damage to town halls in Lille and Grenoble. The same day in Saint-Étienne, a teenager lost his right hand after picking up a police grenade; the prosecutor opened an investigation assigned to the IGPN (TF1/AFP, franceinfo).

The anger behind it is easy to measure. In the first seventeen days of October, diesel in France [averaged €2.365 a litre](https://mon-carburant.com/prix-moyen-carburant-france/). When the yellow vests first took to the streets in November 2018, the figure that shocked the country was [€1.51](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-46233560). AFP notes that fuel has soared since the US-Iran war began this year, that about 45 percent of French people name the cost of living as a major worry, and that a presidential election is six months away. I wrote this morning about [why the ECB is raising rates into this energy shock]({% post_url 2026-10-17-ecb-rate-hikes-energy-shock-lessons %}); this piece is about the street side of the same shock.

What makes France different is not that people get angry about prices. It is that France has a long, well-recorded habit of turning that anger into revolts that governments end up bargaining with.

## 1675: the red caps

In 1675 the treasury of Louis XIV was emptied by the war against the Dutch. His minister Colbert imposed new taxes on tobacco and pewter tableware and a special stamped paper required for legal documents, and he did so in Brittany [without the consent of the province's estates](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/divers/r%C3%A9volte_du_papier_timbr%C3%A9/180595), which broke the autonomy the region had been promised in 1532.

Riots broke out in Rennes in July, where crowds sacked the tobacco and stamped-paper offices. The revolt spread to the countryside of Lower Brittany, where peasants wearing red caps attacked châteaux, burned property deeds and drew up a "Peasant Code", a list of grievances written more than a century before the famous cahiers of 1789.

The answer was savage. The governor, the duc de Chaulnes, had peasants hanged by the hundreds, cut the steeples off rebel churches and occupied Rennes with 5,000 soldiers. Louis XIV granted an amnesty only in February 1676. The tax was the spark; the fuel was a population already crushed by poverty and a sense that its rights had been ignored.

## 2013: the red caps return

The symbol came back three centuries later. In October 2013, Breton farmers, fishermen and food-industry workers put on red caps again to fight an "ecotax" on goods vehicles over 3.5 tonnes, a measure that had been voted with cross-party support and was due to start in January 2014. After a weekend of clashes, Prime Minister Jean-Marc Ayrault [suspended it](https://www.rfi.fr/en/economy/20131029-french-government-suspends-ecotax-after-violent-protests), insisting it was "a suspension, not a cancellation". [France 24](https://www.france24.com/en/20131029-france-ecotax-brittany-farmers-food-climate-taxes-protests) called it a humiliating climb-down that would cost hundreds of millions of euros. It was [later abandoned altogether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonnets_Rouges).

## 2018: the yellow vests

Five years later the trigger was diesel itself. The government had raised fuel taxes by 7.6 cents a litre on diesel in 2018 and planned another 6.5 cents from January 2019, [as part of a push for cleaner cars](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-46233560). President Macron argued that world oil prices explained three quarters of the rise.

On 17 November 2018, [about 282,000 people](https://www.franceinfo.fr/economie/transports/mobilisation-du-17-novembre/video-282-000-manifestants-un-mort-et-227-blesses-le-ministre-de-l-interieur-dresse-le-bilan-de-la-mobilisation-des-gilets-jaunes_3039461.html) blocked roundabouts and roads at more than 2,000 sites, wearing the high-visibility vests every French driver must carry. One woman was killed by a driver at a blockade and 227 people were hurt. Nearly three quarters of respondents in [an Elabe poll](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-46233560) backed the movement.

The government retreated in two steps. On 4 December, Prime Minister Édouard Philippe [suspended the next fuel tax rise](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-46437904) and froze planned gas and electricity increases. On 10 December, Macron called the anger ["deep, and in many ways legitimate"](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-46513189) and [announced](https://www.elysee.fr/emmanuel-macron/2018/12/10/adresse-du-president-de-la-republique-du-lundi-10-decembre-2018) €100 more a month for minimum-wage workers, tax-free overtime and the cancellation of a tax rise for most pensioners. [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/article/uk-france-protests/macrons-concessions-set-to-blow-out-french-deficit-idUKKBN1OA14J/) that the bill came to about €10 billion and pushed France back over the EU's 3 percent deficit limit.

## The pattern

Put the three episodes side by side and the same shape appears.

**The trigger is a cost people see every day.** Stamped paper for every contract, a charge on every lorry, a price on every pump. Taxes hidden in the general budget rarely start revolts; the number at the pump does.

**The grievance is wider than the trigger.** In 1675 it was poverty and broken promises of autonomy. In 2018 the BBC described anger about rural areas being left behind and about living costs in general. This October, protesters talked about wages, police conduct toward students and an election in which they feel unheard.

**Governments retreat late and pay more.** In 2013, France 24 reported that dropping the tax could cost up to €800 million, with the collection equipment already installed. In 2018 a fuel tax worth a few cents became a €10 billion package. In 1675 the crown chose repression, and the province paid in lives.

There is one important difference this time. In 2018 the government could cancel the tax that started it all. In 2026 the price is driven mostly by a war and world markets, which no French minister can cancel, and AFP notes that public debt is projected at more than 120 percent of GDP next year. The cheapest tool from 2018 is not on the table, and the most expensive one is harder to afford.

## My view

I take the grievance seriously. When a civil servant [tells AFP](https://www.afp.com/en/french-protesters-clash-police-during-nationwide-demonstrations) "at the end of the month, I have nothing left", that is not ideology. It is arithmetic, and it falls hardest on people who must drive to work and on families with children.

But I don't accept the romance of the Bastille. Breaking into a town hall, burning a tourism office and torching parked cars hurts ordinary people, often neighbours who share the same complaints, and it hands every opponent of the cause an easy excuse to ignore it. In my judgement, violence costs a movement support it rarely wins back. Both history and decency point the same way: protest hard, and leave the fire alone.

For governments, the lesson of 1675, 2013 and 2018 is to listen before the street forces them to. Help that reaches the households who can't avoid the pump, explained openly and early, costs far less than a retreat in December. France has learned this lesson three times. The coming winter will show whether it remembers.
