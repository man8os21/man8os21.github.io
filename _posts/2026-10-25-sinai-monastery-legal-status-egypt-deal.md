On Saturday 24 October, hours before Egypt's Court of Cassation was due to rule on the lands of Saint Catherine's Monastery, Archbishop Symeon of Sinai [announced](https://www.pagenews.gr/2026/10/24/english-edition/sinai-monastery-moves-toward-egypt-agreement-with-unanimous-orthodox-church-backing/) that he would sign an agreement with the Egyptian state. Greece's foreign minister [called it](https://www.mfa.gr/en/statement-by-the-minister-of-foreign-affairs-george-gerapetritis-on-the-agreement-concerning-saint-catherines-monastery-of-mount-sinai-24-10-2026/) "a historic day" that ends 1,500 years in which the monastery's legal status was "uncertain and unregulated". Egypt's foreign ministry [welcomed it too](https://www.egypttoday.com/Article/1/150236/Egypt-welcomes-archbishop%E2%80%99s-approval-of-contract-on-St-Catherine%E2%80%99s-Monastery), noting that Egypt had cared for the monastery "for more than 1,600 years".

Both sides are pleased, and both frame the same history differently. To see why a desert monastery needed a contract at all, you have to start in the sixth century.

## A fortress for monks, older than Islam

The monastery was built by the Byzantine emperor Justinian around the site Christians identify as the Burning Bush of Moses. Roof beams in its church honour Justinian and his "late" empress Theodora, which lets historians date its completion to between her death in 548 and his in 565, as a [University of Michigan expedition](https://lsa.umich.edu/kelsey/research/past-field-projects/monastery-st-catharine.html) established. That is a few decades before the rise of Islam. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/954) calls it the oldest Christian monastery still used for its original purpose, and the whole area, Mount Sinai included, is sacred to Jews, Christians and Muslims.

The monastery survived because it fitted into every empire that ruled the desert. Inside its walls stands a [12th-century Fatimid mosque](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/en/world-heritage/saint-catherine-s-monastery-and-its-surrounding-area/). Its library keeps a 16th-century copy of the Achtiname, a document that, according to tradition, the Prophet Muhammad granted the monks as a promise of protection; the [Saint Catherine Foundation](https://www.saintcatherinefoundation.org/achtinames-project) itself describes the original as legend. The library also once held most of the Codex Sinaiticus, a fourth-century Greek Bible. A German scholar took it to Russia in 1859 with a written promise to return it; instead it was [sold to the British Museum in 1933](https://asia.si.edu/research/essays/codex-sinaiticus/), and 347 of its leaves are now in London.

That last story explains a lot of the anxiety around Sinai. The monks have lived for centuries on promises, privileges and goodwill rather than on title deeds.

## Why modern law found a gap

Custom works until a modern state asks who owns what. Egypt's Sinai was occupied by Israel from 1967 until [April 1982](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1982/04/25/Israel-returns-Sinai-to-Egypt/1136388558800/), and since then Cairo has guarded the peninsula closely. Under [Decree-Law 14 of 2012](https://leap.unep.org/en/countries/eg/national-legislation/decree-law-no14-2012-concerning-integrated-development-sinai), land in Sinai can generally be owned only by Egyptians; foreigners can get rights of use, but not title, according to a [summary by the Cairo law firm GLA](https://www.glaco.com/blog/ownership-in-sinai-peninsula-and-its-effect-on-ma-transactions/). That rule is not aimed at monks; it applies to everyone.

In 2015, Egyptian authorities sued over dozens of plots the monastery used, [Egypt Independent reports](https://www.egyptindependent.com/agreement-ends-st-catherines-monastery-dispute-between-egypt-and-greece/). In late May 2025 the Ismailia Court of Appeal ruled. Sources differ on the exact day (Egypt's foreign ministry says 25 May, while the Egyptian daily [Al-Dostor](https://www.dostor.org/5086879) and the archbishop's statement say 28 May), but not on the substance. The monks may use the monastery and the religious and archaeological sites, but the state owns them as public property. Contracts the monastery had signed with the local council were upheld, while the remaining plots were declared nature reserves and state land. According to an [unofficial Greek translation](https://www.romfea.gr/diafora/70019-i-apofasi-tou-aigyptiakoy-dikastiriou-gia-tin-i-m-sina), the court also rejected the archbishop's appeal partly because he lacked legal standing, since the monastery was not a legal person, and it ordered eviction from some agricultural land.

Cairo said the monastery would not be closed and that worship was protected. Many in Greece heard the opposite: monks reduced to guests on their own land.

## Two crises at once

The ruling split the monks. In July 2025 the brotherhood voted to remove its archbishop of 52 years, Damianos, and the [Patriarchate of Jerusalem](https://en.jerusalem-patriarchate.info/synodical-decisions/the-holy-synod-decisions-on-the-issue-concerning-the-holy-monastery-of-sinai/) confirmed his departure that September. Symeon was [elected with 19 of 20 votes](https://en.parapolitika.gr/greece/55655/sinai-monastery-elects-new-abbot-simeon-papadopoulos/). Greece meanwhile passed [Law 5224/2025](https://religiousaffairs.minedu.gov.gr/images/Files/tmima_ekklisiastikis_dioikisis_agglika/%CE%9D.5224-2025%20Part%20A_Sinai_eng.pdf), creating an Athens-based public body to represent the monastery and manage its assets in Greece, which does not change anything on Egyptian soil.

The Greek newspaper [To Vima](https://www.tovima.com/world/white-smoke-for-the-sinai-monastery/) describes a year of hard talks, with parts of Egypt's justice ministry arguing that the monastery is "exclusively an internal Egyptian matter" needing no agreement, and the Cassation ruling reportedly expected to go against the monks. That expectation comes from Greek sources and could not be independently confirmed. Symeon then asked the Patriarchs of Jerusalem, Constantinople and Alexandria and the Churches of Cyprus and Greece for their view; all five backed signing within four days.

## What the deal reportedly says

The text has not been published, which matters. According to To Vima, it rests on four pillars. Egypt recognises the monastery as distinct, recognises its founding charter and must protect its monastic life. Religious properties are given to the monks for use free of charge and in perpetuity, while non-religious ones are granted for use under Egyptian law. The monastery accepts Egyptian law and courts. Both sides withdraw their appeals and settle future disputes by dialogue. Greece adds that the monks get long-term residence permits.

Notice what is missing: ownership. The deal appears to work inside the court's ruling, not against it.

## The case against

Former archbishop Damianos calls the settlement "shameful and unacceptable", [Al Manassa reports](https://manassa.news/en/news/34607). He questions whether Symeon may sign without the whole brotherhood, objects that he never saw the draft, and accuses the Greek government of pressure. Some monks reportedly ask why the signing is rushed. Their core argument is serious: a perpetual right of use is still a right granted by the state, and what one government grants, a later one could narrow.

## The people nobody negotiated with

There is a third party. The Jebeleya Bedouin, traditionally said to descend from guards Justinian sent to protect the monks and now mostly Muslim, live around the monastery and work as its guides. Since 2021 Egypt's "Great Transfiguration" project has brought hotels, villas and roads to the area. The [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c707kx2nk7go) and [New Lines](https://newlinesmag.com/spotlight/egypts-sinai-megaproject-is-erasing-bedouin-culture/) reported homes demolished with little or no compensation and graves moved for a car park. Egypt presents the project as a gift to the world; [Al-Ahram](https://gate.ahram.org.eg/News/5676316.aspx) reported in June 2026 that many components were about 97.5% complete, at a cost of about 25 billion Egyptian pounds.

UNESCO's World Heritage Committee has been worried. In [2025](https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/8893/) it urged that further works stay halted, and in [2026](https://whc.unesco.org/fr/decisions/9061/) it welcomed a presidential decree from September 2025 suspending the project until its heritage requirements are met. A technical review found that parts already built could have significant permanent negative effects. An expert mission visited in July 2026, and its report is due in 2027.

## My opinion

I think signing is the lesser risk. A monastery with no legal personality, facing a court that may rule against it, is in a weaker position than one with a contract Egypt's government has publicly welcomed. Egypt's insistence on sovereignty over Sinai is not a pretext either; it comes from a peninsula that was occupied within living memory. And the Greek government's interest in good relations with Cairo, which its minister calls a "profound strategic relationship", is a real constraint, not a betrayal.

But the critics are right about one thing: a deal no one outside a small circle has read cannot be judged, and calling it historic before the text is public asks for trust instead of evidence. The real test will come later. Publish the agreement, and let the world see whether the Bedouin who guarded this place for centuries get the same respect the monks are getting now.
