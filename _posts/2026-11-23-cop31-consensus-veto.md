After more than two weeks and two days of overtime, the longest UN climate summit on record ended in Antalya on 22 November with no main agreement. The draft "Antalya pact", written by the Turkish and Australian co-presidencies, did not mention fossil fuels once. The [European Union, the UK, Norway, Panama, the Marshall Islands and others refused it](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/22/cop31-climate-talks-set-to-end-in-deep-division-as-key-deal-rejected), and Turkey's environment minister Murat Kurum, presiding, [told the hall that "there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted."](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/11/1168855)

That last sentence explains the whole outcome. A UN climate summit cannot pass its main decision by a majority. It needs consensus, and consensus means no country formally objecting. So why are the climate talks built this way, who benefits, and would voting actually be better?

## What was on the table

The draft was not empty. According to [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/3fec1b0a85a0/sustainability/cop/cop31-climate-talks-close-final-deal-says-summit-president-2026-11-21/), it acknowledged that warming is projected to overshoot 1.5°C, called for faster development of carbon-removal technology to reverse that overshoot, called for a tripling of adaptation finance without saying from what baseline, and included a Turkish goal of raising electricity's share of world final energy use to 35% by 2035, from around 23% now.

Not everything died with it. Separate decisions on other agenda items were adopted; China's delegation head Li Gao [told Chinese media](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-11-23/China-urges-faith-in-multilateral-climate-process-after-COP31-1SiAIZnjtOU/p.html) that decisions on mitigation, adaptation and finance let "the multilateral process move forward". But the question of setting up a just-transition mechanism for workers and communities was [pushed to COP32](https://www.citepa.org/transition-juste-quelles-sont-les-avancees-a-la-cop-31/), which Ethiopia hosts in Addis Ababa in November 2027.

What the main text left out was the phrase most countries had already accepted two years earlier. At COP28 in Dubai in 2023, all parties agreed to contribute to ["transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in a just, orderly and equitable manner"](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/cma5%5Fauv%5F4%5Fgst.pdf). In Antalya, [Saudi Arabia and other big oil producers lobbied to keep fossil fuels out of the text](https://www.reuters.com/3fec1b0a85a0/sustainability/cop/cop31-climate-talks-close-final-deal-says-summit-president-2026-11-21/), observers said, and [CBC reported](https://www.cbc.ca/news/world/cop31-talks-collapse-fossil-fuel-9.7431847) that Russia also opposed naming them. The EU's answer, delivered by Ireland's minister Darragh O'Brien, was that it would "not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuels".

The co-presidents were themselves awkward brokers. Australia, which ran the negotiations, is [the world's second-largest exporter of thermal coal and supplies about a fifth of global LNG exports](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/08/chris-bowen-cop31-experts-condemn-australia-coal-gas-approvals); its minister Chris Bowen has argued that other countries still need that coal and gas during their transitions.

So the blockage ran both ways. Oil producers could keep the words out; the EU and its allies could keep the text from passing. Under consensus, each side had a veto.

## A voting rule that was never adopted

This is not how most UN bodies work. Most UN rules of procedure say decisions should be taken by consensus where possible, but allow a "last resort" vote when every effort has failed. The climate convention was meant to have one too. Draft rule 42, prepared for the first COP in 1995, provided for a two-thirds majority as a last resort, as [Carbon Brief's history of the rule](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-the-challenge-of-consensus-decision-making-in-un-climate-negotiations) explains.

It was never agreed. Before COP1, OPEC members, mainly Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, with Nigeria, Iran and others, argued that substantive decisions should only be taken by consensus. Carbon Brief's account adds that they were advised by US-based lobbyists linked to fossil-fuel and car industries. Because the rules themselves had to be adopted by consensus, the dispute could not be outvoted either. Since 1995 the COP has applied the draft rules every year [with the single exception of rule 42](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/Presiding-Officers-Guide-to-the-UNFCCC-Process.pdf). The UN's own guide for presiding officers says plainly that decisions are "generally adopted by consensus, as there is no agreed voting rule".

Compare the ozone treaty. Under the Montreal Protocol, if consensus fails, adjustments to controls can be adopted by [a two-thirds majority that must include a majority of both developing and developed parties](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/articles/article-2-control-measures). The climate regime has nothing like it.

## Consensus is not unanimity, until it is

How strict consensus is depends on the person holding the gavel. In Copenhagen in 2009, Bolivia, Venezuela, Sudan and Tuvalu objected to the Copenhagen Accord, so the conference only ["took note" of it](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/19122009/conference-parties-takes-note-copenhagen-accord/) instead of adopting it. A year later in Cancún, Mexico's Patricia Espinosa gavelled the deal through over Bolivia's explicit objection, saying that ["the rule of consensus does not mean unanimity"](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/11122010/climate-talks-end-modest-deal-and-standing-ovation/) and does not let one delegation impose a veto.

The lesson from those two years is that a president can override one lone objector, but not a large bloc. In Antalya the objectors included the EU, the UK, Norway and several small island and least-developed countries. No president could have called that consensus.

## Why not just vote?

Reform has been tried. In 2011, [Papua New Guinea and Mexico proposed amending the convention](https://unfccc.int/resource/docs/2011/cop17/eng/04r01.pdf) so that, once consensus efforts failed, decisions could pass by a three-quarters majority. The proposal was discussed, deferred and never adopted.

There are serious reasons it stalled, and not all of them come from oil states.

**Consensus protects the small.** In a COP plenary, Tuvalu's objection counts as much as China's, and many small states value that.

**The South was split, not united behind Europe.** Small islands such as the Marshall Islands objected. But the Africa group, China and Pakistan [did not object](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/22/cop31-climate-talks-set-to-end-in-deep-division-as-key-deal-rejected), despite reservations. Pakistan argued that "climate action is not a menu from which we can select only what suits us", and Ghana said Africa "has always chosen to build multilateralism rather than walk away from it". China's delegation said "mutual accusations will not help build trust". A vote would not end that split; it would only decide who wins it.

**A vote does not make anyone act.** Under the Paris Agreement, national climate plans are set by each country. A text passed over the objection of Saudi Arabia, Russia or a large emerging economy would not oblige them to cut a barrel of production. It could even push them to disengage, as the United States has done: Washington sent [no official delegation to COP31](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/nov/22/cop31-turkey-no-official-us-delegation-second-year-row/), for the second year running.

**The producers' fears are real, even if one disagrees with them.** The [IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/cr/2026/english/1sauea2026001.pdf) notes that oil still provides more than half of Saudi Arabia's fiscal revenue. For a government whose budget rests on one commodity, a global text that names that commodity as the problem looks like a threat to its social contract. Its delegate in Antalya argued that it "makes no sense" to take carbon removal "off the table" if the climate is so urgent.

**Voting could cut against the EU too.** Developing countries are a majority of parties. A voting rule would let them outvote rich countries on finance, which is exactly the issue on which many of them felt let down in Antalya. Kenya complained that "Africa is not named" in the text, and the Philippines said "a target without means is a promise without a pathway".

## Do the words even matter?

A fair sceptic would ask whether any of this changes emissions. The evidence cuts both ways. Two years after every government accepted "transitioning away" in Dubai, the [2025 Production Gap Report](https://productiongap.org/2025report/) found that governments still plan to produce more than double the fossil fuels in 2030 that a 1.5°C path allows, with planned coal 500% above it, gas 92% and oil 31%. Words in a COP text clearly do not stop drilling.

Yet the process has not been useless. When the Paris Agreement was signed in 2015, existing policies pointed to roughly 4°C of warming this century; a decade later the projection is about 2.8°C, [Reuters notes](https://www.reuters.com/3fec1b0a85a0/sustainability/cop/cop31-climate-talks-close-final-deal-says-summit-president-2026-11-21/), and analysts credit the summits with sending signals to investors in renewable power and electric vehicles. The texts work less as orders than as signposts. That is why each side fought so hard over one phrase: dropping it would have told investors the direction was negotiable.

## Clubs of the willing

Some countries have started working around the plenary. In April, Colombia and the Netherlands hosted the first [conference on transitioning away from fossil fuels in Santa Marta](https://www.carbonbrief.org/santa-marta-key-outcomes-from-first-summit-on-transitioning-away-from-fossil-fuels): 57 countries, about a third of the world economy, with China, Russia and the US not invited. Turkey, Antalya's host, was among them.

Clubs can move faster, but they leave out most of the world's emissions. A club without China, India, the US and the Gulf cannot set the global direction.

## Opinion: Saying no was right, but it is not a plan

The EU was right to refuse a climate text that cannot name the main cause of climate change. Accepting it would have quietly weakened the Dubai commitment, and the next presidency would have started from the weaker text.

But the critics from the South have a point that should sting. Power Shift Africa called Antalya ["the worst COP ever"](https://powershiftafrica.org/in-the-news/our-verdict-antalya-has-been-the-worst-cop-ever/) and accused Europe of demanding fossil-fuel roadmaps from others while expanding fossil fuels itself and holding back finance. Australia's export boom shows the same gap between words and deeds among the "ambitious". And several vulnerable countries wanted the deal passed. A no from Brussels costs Brussels little; a weaker finance signal costs a flooded village a lot.

I do not think a simple voting rule would fix this. A two-thirds vote with a double majority, like the ozone treaty's, could help on procedural and finance questions, where the problem is paralysis rather than sovereignty. On fossil fuels, the honest route is the harder one: richer countries showing at home that the transition works, and paying for it abroad, so that saying the word "fossil fuels" no longer looks like a threat to everyone who lives off them.
