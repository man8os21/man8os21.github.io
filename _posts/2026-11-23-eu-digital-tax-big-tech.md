On 21 November, António Costa, who chairs the summits of EU leaders, put a new idea into the fight over the European Union's 2028–2034 budget: a 3% levy on the digital revenues of very large tech companies. Leaders meet in Brussels on 26–27 November to argue about it, alongside cuts that would bring the seven-year budget down to about €1.58 trillion ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/5f374b32b07b/markets/us/eu-leaders-chair-proposes-new-cuts-eu-2028-2034-budget-draft-suggests-digital-2026-11-21/)).

Taxes like this have already set off trade threats between the United States and France, Canada, India and others, and most of the countries that tried them have retreated. So it is worth asking three plain questions: why do governments keep reaching for this tool, who actually ends up paying, and is it worth the fight?

## What exactly is proposed

Under Costa's plan, companies with at least €50 million a year of digital turnover in the EU and more than €750 million worldwide would pay 3% of their annual net turnover from online advertising, digital intermediation (marketplaces, booking and app platforms) and the monetisation of user data ([DW](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-takes-aim-at-tech-giants-in-tax-proposal/a-80009255)). The money would be an EU "own resource": a revenue stream that goes straight to the EU budget, like customs duties or the levy on unrecycled plastic. Every euro raised this way is a euro national treasuries do not have to send to Brussels ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/5f374b32b07b/markets/us/eu-leaders-chair-proposes-new-cuts-eu-2028-2034-budget-draft-suggests-digital-2026-11-21/)).

The European Commission has estimated that a 3% tax of this kind would bring in about €5 billion a year ([Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/antonio-costa-tax-big-tech-eu-budget-trump-midterms/)). That is small next to a budget of more than €200 billion a year.

The Commission's earlier idea, a lump-sum charge on all large companies called CORE, met resistance, especially from Germany, because it would also hit European firms. A levy that falls mainly on foreign digital giants is politically easier, and Politico reports that Berlin has warmed to it ([Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/antonio-costa-tax-big-tech-eu-budget-trump-midterms/)).

## Why governments tax revenue instead of profit

Normally companies are taxed on profit, where they have offices, factories or staff. Digital firms can earn large sums from users in a country while booking most of the profit somewhere else. A digital services tax (DST) gets around this by taxing gross revenue from specific activities in the country where the users are, whether or not the company is profitable there ([PwC](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/tax/newsletters/tax-policy-bulletin/assets/pwc-state-of-play-of-dsts-and-other-similar-measures.pdf)).

France introduced the first 3% DST in 2019, as a stopgap until a global deal. Austria, Italy, Spain, the UK, Turkey (at 7.5%), Kenya and Nepal followed with their own versions ([PwC](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/tax/newsletters/tax-policy-bulletin/assets/pwc-state-of-play-of-dsts-and-other-similar-measures.pdf)). France's tax raised €900.9 million in 2025, more than three times its 2019 yield ([Observatoire du e-commerce](https://observatoire-ecom.com/analyses/taxe-services-numeriques-taux-et-rendement)).

The global deal was supposed to make all this unnecessary. Under "Pillar One" of the OECD talks, part of the profits of the biggest multinationals would have been reallocated to the countries where their customers are, and DSTs would have been withdrawn. That is why the EU shelved its own 2018 digital levy in 2021 ([Reuters, 2021](https://www.reuters.com/business/eu-commission-puts-hold-plan-digital-services-levy-2021-07-12/)). But the multilateral convention never came into force, and according to a 2026 summary by the consultancy Alvarez & Marsal, Washington now regards the original Pillar One as dead ([Alvarez & Marsal](https://akamai.alvarezandmarsal.com/thought-leadership/a-m-tax-policy-insights-february-2026)). Meanwhile the other half of the deal, the 15% global minimum tax, was adjusted in January 2026 so that US-parented groups are exempt from most of its rules ([EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2026-0115-breaking-tax-news-oecd-releases-side-by-side-arrangement-exempting-us-multinationals-from-most-pillar-two-global-minimum-tax-rules)). Seen from Paris or Rome, the bargain looks one-sided: Europe paused its taxes, and the promised reallocation never came.

## Who really pays

The legal taxpayer is the platform. The economic taxpayer is often someone else. When France's tax arrived in 2019, Amazon raised the commission it charges sellers on its French marketplace by 3% ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/blog/amazon-france-digital-tax/)). Google adds a "regulatory operating cost" to advertisers' bills for ads shown in France and Spain; it says the French surcharge will rise from 2% to 3% from 1 January 2027 ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/9750227?hl=en)).

So a large part of a DST tends to land on small sellers, advertisers and, through prices, on consumers, including European ones. A new working paper using French data also finds side effects: traffic fell on pure marketplace sites run by liable groups, rose on purely ad-funded sites, and fell on smaller non-liable sites that depend on ads to bring in visitors ([Sanz-Maldonado, draft paper](https://sanzg0.github.io/job-market-paper.pdf)). It is one study, not yet peer-reviewed.

Supporters reply that pass-through does not make the tax pointless. Even if platforms recover part of it, the money is still collected where the users are, and a common EU rate would replace a patchwork of national ones.

## Why Washington calls it discrimination

The US case is not only about protecting its companies. Because the thresholds and the covered activities match the business models of large American platforms, the burden falls mostly on US firms. The industry group CCIA estimates that the DSTs of the UK, France, Spain, Austria and Italy cost US companies over $13 billion between 2020 and 2025, and argues that this shrinks the US tax base, because DST payments can reduce the profits taxed at home ([CCIA](https://ccianet.org/news/2026/07/ccia-releases-report-on-cost-status-of-key-digital-services-taxes/)). These are a lobby group's figures, but its point that revenue taxes also hit loss-making firms is fair.

Washington has backed the argument with pressure. In Donald Trump's first term, the US trade office found France's tax discriminatory and set 25% tariffs on about $1.3 billion of French goods, then suspended them and finally dropped the actions in 2021 while the OECD talks continued ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2021/11/18/2021-25199/termination-of-actions-in-the-section-301-digital-services-tax-investigations-of-austria-france)). In June 2025 Canada cancelled its DST a day before the first payments were due, to restart trade talks ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2025/06/canada-rescinds-digital-services-tax-to-advance-broader-trade-negotiations-with-the-united-states.html)). India dropped its 6% levy on online advertising from April 2025, at a time of US tariff threats ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/india-proposes-remove-equalisation-levy-digital-services-government-source-says-2025-03-25/)), although its finance minister said the change was part of domestic tax reform, not a response to Washington ([Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/india/equalisation-levy-withdrawal-not-linked-to-trump-tariffs-nirmala-sitharaman-9910234/)).

In June 2026 Trump threatened a 100% tariff on any European country that imposes a DST. CNN noted that markets barely reacted, because a February Supreme Court ruling had removed the emergency-powers route he had used for quick tariffs; the slower Section 301 process remains ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/06/26/business/trump-tariff-threat)). EU supporters of the levy now argue that Republican losses in the November midterms make retaliation less likely ([Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/antonio-costa-tax-big-tech-eu-budget-trump-midterms/)). That is a political bet, not a fact. The EU sold €554 billion of goods to the US in 2025, nearly a third of it medicines ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20260226-1)); a tariff fight would put far more at stake than €5 billion a year.

## Divisions inside Europe

The EU is not united. France, Italy and Spain, which already have national DSTs, have long favoured an EU-wide version ([Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/antonio-costa-tax-big-tech-eu-budget-trump-midterms/)). Ireland, where many US tech firms have their European headquarters, says no. Taoiseach Micheál Martin said "global taxation has to be done globally", that Ireland accepted changes to its corporate tax in the OECD deal on the understanding there would be no digital tax, and that a unilateral levy could reopen the EU–US trade deal ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/ireland/2026/11/21/antonio-costa-revives-eu-digital-tax-proposal-in-bid-to-reach-agreement-on-budget/)). European digital firms are not keen either: Booking.com's chief executive warned that such levies hurt Europe's own economy ([Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/antonio-costa-tax-big-tech-eu-budget-trump-midterms/)). New EU own resources need the unanimous agreement of all member states, so one firm "no" can sink the plan.

## The view from outside the West

For countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America the question is bigger than the EU budget. They host billions of users and earn little tax from them. Many now push for taxing rights over cross-border digital services in the UN negotiations on a new tax convention, which the US has left; the South Centre, a think tank of developing countries, argues for broad source-country rights that do not depend on physical presence ([South Centre](https://www.southcentre.int/sc-inputs-on-workstream-ii-on-taxation-of-services-26-august-2026/)). If even the EU, with all its market power, backs down under US pressure, smaller countries will read it as a lesson.

## Scenarios

1. **The levy is dropped or postponed.** Ireland or another state blocks it, or leaders fear a trade fight. The budget gap is closed by cuts or higher national contributions.
2. **It is agreed in principle but delayed.** The Council paper calls for a directive in 2027 and collection from the following year ([Council of the EU](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/11/21/multiannual-financial-framework-president-costa-s-proposal/)), leaving time to trade it away in talks with Washington, as happened with Pillar One.
3. **It goes ahead and the US retaliates** through Section 301 tariffs aimed at European exports.
4. **It goes ahead and replaces national DSTs**, giving companies one EU rate instead of several.

## Opinion: a fair aim, a weak tool, and a bad moment to bluff

The principle behind the levy is reasonable. When a company earns heavily from a market's users, that market has a fair claim to tax some of that income, and the global deal that was meant to do this has stalled. Europe has a right to stop waiting.

But a 3% tax on revenue is a blunt instrument. A good part of it will be passed on to European sellers and advertisers, it hits low-margin businesses harder than very profitable ones, and €5 billion a year is small next to the trade it could put at risk. Its main value may be as leverage to restart a real deal on where profits are taxed. Leverage only works if it is credible, and a proposal that one member state can veto is not very credible yet. If the EU wants this, it should agree first on what it would trade the levy for, and prepare for retaliation, rather than discovering its red lines in the middle of a tariff fight.
