On 27 October the European Union's gender equality agency, EIGE, published a short brief with an uncomfortable message. Europe has spent a decade estimating how many girls living here are at risk of female genital mutilation (FGM). The numbers are real and they matter. But, the agency says, they cannot tell a teacher, a doctor or a social worker which girl is actually in danger.

That sounds like a statistical footnote. It is really a question about how a state protects children without treating whole communities as suspects.

## What FGM is, and how big it is

The World Health Organization [defines FGM](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/female-genital-mutilation) as all procedures that partly or totally remove the external female genitalia, or otherwise injure the female genital organs, for non-medical reasons. It has no health benefits. The forms range from a symbolic nick to infibulation, where the cut edges of the labia are sewn together and must be reopened for sex or childbirth. The consequences can include severe bleeding, infection, infertility, childbirth complications and post-traumatic stress, according to [UNICEF's 2024 data report](https://data.unicef.org/resources/female-genital-mutilation-a-global-concern-2024/), which opens with three girls in Sierra Leone presumed to have bled to death after an initiation in January 2024. [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/female-genital-mutilation) puts the cost of treating its complications at about US$1.4 billion a year in the countries it studied.

UNICEF [estimated in March 2024](https://www.unicef.org/press-releases/over-230-million-girls-and-women-alive-today-have-been-subjected-female-genital) that more than 230 million girls and women alive today have undergone it: over 144 million in Africa, over 80 million in Asia and about 6 million in the Middle East. That is 30 million more than the 2016 estimate. Asia's share is often forgotten: in Indonesia, about half of women aged 15 to 49 have been cut, according to [UNICEF's country profile](https://data.unicef.org/wp-content/uploads/cp/fgm/FGM_IDN.pdf), and the government only [formally banned the practice in 2024](https://www.unfpa.org/fgm-country-snapshots/2024-indonesia), including when health workers do it. The rise does not mean the practice is spreading. As [UN News reported](https://news.un.org/en/story/2024/03/1147402), it reflects the fact that the population of girls in practising countries is growing quickly. Prevalence is falling, but the decline would need to be 27 times faster to meet the UN goal of ending FGM by 2030.

Europe is a small part of this picture. The figures EIGE cites say more than 600,000 women and girls in Europe live with the consequences, and around 190,000 girls may be at risk in the 17 EU countries that have studies.

## Where the 190,000 comes from

The method, which EIGE developed in 2015, starts with surveys in countries where FGM is practised. If a large share of women in a country of origin have been cut, girls in Europe whose families come from there are treated as potentially exposed, and the estimate is then adjusted for things like time since migration. The result is a range, not a count. EIGE's [country pages](https://eige.europa.eu/gender-based-violence/female-genital-mutilation/risk-estimations?language_content_entity=en) show how wide those ranges are: in Italy, 15% to 24% of about 76,000 girls from practising countries were estimated to be at risk; in Greece, 25% to 42% of about 1,800 girls.

The new brief, based on [50 peer-reviewed studies and 32 policy sources](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13970/17/european-institute-for-gender-equality-calls-for-better-assessment-of-female-genital-mutilation-risks-in-europe), says this starting point is necessary but not enough. As the [2eu.brussels summary](https://2eu.brussels/en/news/eige-warns-female-genital-mutilation-risk-in-the-eu-cannot-be-estimated-from-country-of-origin-alone) puts it, a population estimated to be potentially exposed is not a count of future cases, and a girl's origin or community does not, on its own, show that she faces a threat. The 190,000 figure is also not new: it comes from a 2024 map by the End FGM European Network, which combines studies with different methods, years and age groups.

## Why the risk changes after migration

The core finding is that risk is not fixed. Migration can lower it: schooling, legal protection, economic independence and trusted services help families say no. It can also raise it: isolation, insecurity, pressure from relatives back home and the fear of a daughter being seen as "unmarriageable". EIGE names moments when danger rises, such as puberty, marriage talks, recent arrival and trips to the country of origin.

Research in Sweden points the same way. A [BMJ Open study](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-017506) of Somali migrants found that opposition to all forms of cutting grew the longer people had lived in the country. France offers the starkest number. According to [The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/europe/the-french-way-a-better-approach-to-fighting-fgm-9006369.html), examinations in the early 1980s suggested an 80% chance that the daughter of a cut mother would also be cut; a 2007 survey put it at 11%. That fall happened alongside prosecutions, prevention and a generation growing up in France, so no one can say how much each contributed.

## Does the criminal law work?

[Directive (EU) 2024/1385](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1385/oj/eng) sets a common floor. By 14 June 2027 member states must make it a specific offence, including coercing or procuring a girl to undergo it, with a maximum sentence of at least five years. The [Commission adds](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/E-10-2025-000669-ASW_EN.html) that the limitation period must not start before the victim turns 18, and that states must train health workers, support victims and collect data.

The record of prosecutions is strikingly uneven. France, which has no FGM-specific law and uses its general offence of violence causing permanent mutilation, has held [around 40 cases](https://www.fgmcri.org/media/uploads/Law%20Reports/france_law_report_v3_(august_2023).pdf), most ending in convictions. Much of that is owed to the lawyer Linda Weil-Curiel, who told [El País](https://elpais.com/elpais/2019/02/04/planeta_futuro/1549285616_641917.html) she pushed for these cases to be tried in France's highest criminal courts, and who credits trials with a deterrent effect. The same article noted that Britain, which has had a specific law since 1985, had secured its first conviction only in 2019. Years without a single conviction are what pushed British officials towards tougher tools, including a [mandatory reporting duty](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/view/journals/crsw/13/2/article-p176.xml) for professionals from 2015, in the expectation that it would uncover hidden cases.

## The case against heavy-handed protection

There is a serious argument on the other side, and it comes largely from the communities concerned. In [focus groups in Bristol](https://theconversation.com/british-somalis-and-fgm-everybody-is-a-suspect-you-are-guilty-until-proven-innocent-129827), British Somalis who said they were committed to ending FGM described being referred to social services for asking to take a holiday in term time, unannounced home visits by police, and midwives questioning them about FGM at every appointment. One said: "Everybody is a suspect. You are guilty until you are proven innocent." Some avoided medical care as a result.

A [2025 study](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/view/journals/crsw/13/2/article-p176.xml) using freedom-of-information requests found that the 20 of 43 police force areas in England and Wales that answered recorded 390 FGM-related offences under the mandatory reporting duty between 2015 and 2022, many of which ended with no suspect or no action. Its authors argue this does not show the hidden crime wave the policy assumed.

A more radical critique comes from the Sierra Leonean anthropologist Fuambai Ahmadu, herself an initiated woman. In [a 2009 interview](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8322.2009.00699.x) she argued that campaigns impose outside assumptions about women's bodies and sexuality and ignore what initiated women say about their own lives. Others have proposed harm reduction instead of abolition. In 2010 the American Academy of Pediatrics briefly suggested that doctors might offer a ritual "nick" to stop families travelling for worse procedures, then [withdrew the statement](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/722840) within weeks after protests. WHO's position is that no form is acceptable, including when a doctor does it. Religion is contested too: the Gambian MP who tried to lift his country's ban invoked religious duty, while, as [Reuters noted](https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/gambia-parliament-rejects-bill-unban-female-genital-mutilation-speaker-says-2024-07-15/), many Islamic scholars dispute that.

## Africa is moving, unevenly

It would be wrong to imagine that the push against FGM comes only from Europe. [UNICEF says](https://news.un.org/en/story/2024/03/1147402) around 400 million people in practising countries in Africa and the Middle East, two-thirds of the population, oppose it, and half of 30 years' progress came in the last decade, with Kenya, Sierra Leone and Egypt as examples. In July 2024 [Gambia's parliament rejected](https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/gambia-parliament-rejects-bill-unban-female-genital-mutilation-speaker-says-2024-07-15/) a bill that would have made it the first country to repeal an FGM ban, after what the [African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights](https://achpr.au.int/en/news/press-releases/2024-07-17/womens-amendment-act-2015-decriminalizing-female-genital-mutilation) credited to Gambian women's groups, lawyers and religious and traditional leaders. In 2025 the regional ECOWAS court [ruled against Sierra Leone](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2025/jul/28/the-matter-is-in-his-hands-alone-president-of-sierra-leone-urged-to-ban-fgm-as-court-rules-it-tantamount-to-torture) for failing to ban it.

The new worry is doctors. [WHO said in 2025](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-04-2025-who-issues-new-recommendations-to-end-the-rise-in--medicalized--female-genital-mutilation-and-support-survivors) that about one in four survivors, some 52 million, were cut by a health worker, which can make the practice look legitimate.

## Opinion: protect the girl, not the statistic

*This section is my opinion.*

I think EIGE is right, and that both sides of this argument need to hear it. To the communities: the knife is real, the girls in Sierra Leone who bled to death were real, and the law is right to treat it as violence against a child. To the authorities: a mother's origin is not evidence. A system that questions every Somali woman at every check-up teaches her to stop coming, and then nobody is there on the day she, or her daughter, actually needs help.

The answer is not less protection but sharper protection: trained professionals who know the real warning signs, community workers whom families trust, and investigation when there is an actual sign of harm. The girls at risk need adults who notice. They do not need to be counted in a range of 15% to 24%.
