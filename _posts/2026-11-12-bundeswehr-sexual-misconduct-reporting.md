On 11 November 2026, Germany's defence minister Boris Pistorius stood in the Bundestag and presented numbers his ministry had been sitting on for almost a year. Four in ten women serving in the Bundeswehr said they had experienced some form of sexual misconduct in the previous twelve months. Over their whole careers, 72% said they had been sexually harassed with words at least once. And, in the finding Pistorius himself called the decisive one, [only one case in three to one in six had ever been reported](https://www.dw.com/de/bundeswehr-studie-zeigt-ausma%C3%9F-sexueller-bel%C3%A4stigung-in-der-truppe/a-79919828).

The second number explains the first. An army that hears about a fraction of what happens will keep believing it has a small problem.

## What the study actually measured

The survey is a "dark-field" study, the German term for research into crimes that never reach the authorities. The Bundeswehr's Centre of Military History and Social Sciences in Potsdam wrote to all servicewomen and a roughly equal number of men, about 45,000 people in total, and [some 8,100 completed the anonymous online questionnaire](https://www.tagesschau.de/investigativ/ndr-wdr/bundeswehr-sexuelle-belaestigung-100.html) between 13 July and 27 August, according to [POLITICO](https://www.politico.eu/article/study-finds-widespread-bundeswehr-sexual-misconduct/). The response rate was about 18%. The researchers weighted the answers to match the make-up of the armed forces and consider the results representative. The study was [commissioned by Eva Högl](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/11/over-60-of-women-in-german-military-are-sexually-harassed-study-finds), then the Bundestag's Parliamentary Commissioner for the Armed Forces.

Headlines of "more than 60%" or "75%" refer to women's whole careers; the 40% figure covers the last twelve months.

The study sorted misconduct into four levels. In the previous twelve months, [according to ZDF](https://www.zdfheute.de/politik/deutschland/bundeswehr-dunkelfeldstudie-sexuelle-belaestigung-boris-pistorius-100.html):

- 38% of women and 16% of men experienced verbal sexual harassment, such as lewd remarks;
- 7% of women and 5% of men experienced visual harassment, including images taken without consent or altered with AI;
- 9% of women and 3% of men experienced unwanted physical contact;
- 2% of women and 2% of men experienced sexual violence.

The people most at risk were young women in the lowest ranks and, among men, soldiers who are not heterosexual. The perpetrators were overwhelmingly men. Ship crews were hit harder. And women in units where more than 15% of personnel were women [reported less misconduct](https://www.dw.com/de/bundeswehr-studie-zeigt-ausma%C3%9F-sexueller-bel%C3%A4stigung-in-der-truppe/a-79919828).

Women make up a little over 13% of Germany's roughly 186,000 soldiers, [according to July 2026 personnel figures](https://augengeradeaus.net/2026/08/personalstaerke-juli-2026-hoechster-stand-seit-2013-ministerium-sieht-sich-im-zielkorridor/). So on average, servicewomen work in a force where they are still below the share at which the study found misconduct falls.

## Why people stay silent

Reporting rates varied by severity. [Only about one in five](https://www.stern.de/politik/deutschland/studie-der-bundeswehr--sexuelles-fehlverhalten-massiv-verbreitet-38533146.html) non-physical incidents was reported. One in four women reported unwanted touching. Even sexual violence was reported by women in only one case in three, and by men in one in five. Men are not a side note here: the share reporting sexual violence was the same 2% for both sexes, and because men are the large majority of soldiers, that means more male victims in absolute numbers.

The most common reason given was not wanting to draw attention. Depending on the category, between 36% and 61% of non-reporters [said they feared negative consequences for themselves](https://www.tagesschau.de/investigativ/ndr-wdr/bundeswehr-sexuelle-belaestigung-100.html), and between 44% and 59% doubted a report would achieve anything. Pistorius said the decisive gap is "trust in the reporting channels."

The root is structural. In most armies a complaint goes up the chain of command, to a superior who knows both people, assesses both careers and looks worse if his unit has a scandal.

## Germany is not unusual

Germany is unusual mainly in having measured it.

**Norway**, which conscripts women as well as men and is often held up as a model, runs a survey every two years. In 2024 [about half of female conscripts, students and young female employees](https://www.ffi.no/en/publications-archive/mobbing-og-seksuell-trakassering-i-forsvaret-i-fma-og-ved-ffi-resultater-fra-en-sporreundersokelse-i-2024) in the armed forces reported sexual harassment in the previous year. That is still an improvement: in 2018 the figure for female privates and students [was almost 78%](https://www.forsvaretsforum.no/kultur-mobbing-og-seksuell-trakassering-personell/nesten-annenhver-kvinne-i-forstegangstjeneste-opplever-trakassering/439414). The share of people reporting barely changed between 2022 and 2024, and the reasons for silence are the same as in Germany.

**The United States** has surveyed its forces for decades. In 2023, [6.8% of active-duty women](https://www.sapr.mil/sites/default/files/public/docs/reports/AR/FY23/FY23_Annual_Report.pdf) reported unwanted sexual contact in the past year, down from 8.4% in 2021, [the first decline in nearly ten years](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3777528/department-of-defense-releases-fiscal-year-2023-annual-report-on-sexual-assault/). About a quarter of victims reported to an authority, up from a fifth. Yet only 38% of active-duty women [said they trusted the department to protect their privacy](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/pentagon-congress/2024/05/16/military-sexual-assault-totals-down-but-trust-among-women-remains-low/) if they reported.

**Ukraine** has little official data; a small 2020 study by the Invisible Battalion project [of 165 service members](https://invisiblebattalion.org/finalna-pres-konferencziya-po-proyektu-nevydymyj-bataljon-3-0-seksualni-domagannya-u-vijskovij-sferi-v-ukrayini/) found widespread harassment and very low reporting. For most of the world's large armies, including Russia's, China's and India's, I could find no comparable public survey. An army with no numbers is not an army with no problem.

## Three ways to fix reporting

**A channel outside the hierarchy.** Germany already has one of the oldest: any soldier [may write directly to the Parliamentary Commissioner for the Armed Forces](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/wehrbbtg/__7.html) without going through official channels, and may not be disciplined or disadvantaged for doing so. Yet in 2025 the commissioner [received only 46 such submissions](https://table.media/assets/berlin/wehrbericht-2026-(1).pdf) on sexual misconduct, and the ministry logged 430 initial reports, in a force where the survey implies thousands of incidents a year. An external door does not help much if people do not know it exists or do not believe walking through it is safe. France created the [Thémis cell](https://www.terremag.defense.gouv.fr/enquetes/tim-vous-explique/la-mission-de-la-cellule-themis) in 2014, inside the armed forces' inspectorate, after a book called *La Guerre invisible* exposed sexual violence in the French military. Victims and witnesses can go to it without passing through their commanders. Germany has just set up an independent counselling service called HELP, which [will be reachable around the clock](https://www.stern.de/politik/deutschland/studie-der-bundeswehr--sexuelles-fehlverhalten-massiv-verbreitet-38533146.html). Green MP Sara Nanni wants [an external office to receive formal reports](https://www.focus.de/politik/deutschland/60-prozent-der-soldatinnen-geben-sexuelle-belaestigungen-an_5f1b4b54-615f-4a91-8d31-3f698919c8cb.html).

**Taking prosecution away from commanders.** Since December 2023, US commanders no longer decide whether sexual assault is prosecuted; independent military lawyers in Offices of Special Trial Counsel do. In its first full year, the Army's office [reviewed more than 8,600 investigations, took over 2,172, and preferred charges in 489](https://www.army.mil/article/282649/the_army_office_of_special_trial_counsel_marks_its_first_anniversary). South Korea went further after Air Force master sergeant Lee Ye-ram took her own life in 2021; after she reported a sexual assault, [superiors allegedly pressed her to settle](https://en.yna.co.kr/view/AEN20210604004152325) instead of protecting her, and the Air Force chief offered to resign. From July 2022, [sex crimes by soldiers go to civilian courts](https://en.yna.co.kr/view/AEN20210831008300325). Canada, after a review by former Supreme Court justice Louise Arbour found serious failings in how the military handled misconduct, passed [Bill C-11 in 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/news/2026/06/bill-c-11-receives-royal-assent-bringing-new-military-justice-reforms-into-force.html), removing the military's power to investigate and prosecute sexual offences committed in Canada.

**Making leaders answer for their units.** Pistorius's 34-point plan [makes how a superior handles reported cases a fixed criterion in his own assessment](https://www.stern.de/politik/deutschland/studie-der-bundeswehr--sexuelles-fehlverhalten-massiv-verbreitet-38533146.html). "Whoever is not a role model, tolerates misconduct or does not deal with it consistently will no longer hold a leadership position," he said.

## The case for the chain of command, and the evidence against easy answers

Many commanders argue that discipline cannot be outsourced: an officer responsible for cohesion needs authority to act fast, and moving cases elsewhere lets leaders call the problem "someone else's job". Civilian courts are also slow and unfamiliar with military life.

Canada shows that both sides have a point. Ottawa ordered transfers to civilian police in 2021, yet military police [kept 595 of 879 sexual offence cases](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/military-police-sexual-offence-investigations-9.7248030), citing victims' preferences and practical obstacles; civilian police declined 69 of those they did receive, and two cases were stayed in civilian court in 2023 for unreasonable delay. Canada's Conservatives wanted victims to be able to choose between military and civilian justice, [an amendment the government dropped](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/bill-c-11-military-sexual-misconduct-recommendation-9.7191845). Moving a case out of the barracks does not by itself make justice faster or more likely.

Nor is there yet strong evidence that external channels raise reporting on their own. The US reporting rate rose from about a fifth to a quarter between 2021 and 2023, but that was mostly before its new prosecutors took over cases at the end of 2023, so the reform cannot take the credit. Norway, with years of surveys and campaigns, saw harassment fall but the share of people reporting stay almost flat. What seems to matter more is slower: who leads units, how many women serve in them, and whether earlier complaints led to anything.

The German study has limits too. With an 18% response rate, people with experiences to tell may have been more likely to answer, though the most afraid may also have stayed away. And each country's survey defines harassment differently, so comparisons are rough.

## Opinion: count again, and take the complaint out of the unit

Germany deserves credit for publishing this. Germany wants more women in uniform, and the 15% finding suggests that recruiting them is itself part of the cure, not a reason to hide the numbers.

What will matter most is whether a 20-year-old corporal can report a superior without that superior, or his friends, having any say over her complaint or her career. HELP is a counselling line, not a reporting office. Germany should make its existing external channel, the parliamentary commissioner, known to every recruit and give it the staff to investigate, add a formal external reporting office alongside the HELP line, keep commanders accountable for prevention so they cannot wash their hands of it, and learn from Canada that a transfer on paper means little without the capacity behind it. Then it should repeat the survey, as Pistorius has promised, and judge itself by one number above all: the share of victims who decide that reporting is worth it.
