Almost everyone has met them. The countdown timer that resets when you reload the page. The "only 2 left!" banner. The free trial that quietly becomes a paid subscription. The cancel button hidden four menus deep, and the pop-up that asks "Are you sure you want to miss out?" when you finally find it. Designers call these tricks **dark patterns**. On Wednesday 11 November 2026 the European Commission presents a law meant to ban many of them, the [Digital Fairness Act](https://www.thejournal.ie/digital-fairness-act-michael-mcgrath-7221750-Nov2026/) (DFA).

The details below come from drafts seen by [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/09/eu-to-blacklist-ten-manipulative-online-design-traps-leaked-draft-shows), [Reuters](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/companies-risk-fines-of-6-global-turnover-for-unfair-digital-trade-practices-eu-draft-law-shows-5012950) and [POLITICO](https://www.politico.eu/article/brussels-takes-on-the-evils-of-online-commerce-digital-fairness-act-commission/) before publication. The official text may differ, and it is only a proposal: the European Parliament and the 27 governments must agree on it, which usually takes at least a year.

## What the draft would do

The draft would replace the EU's 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a regulation that applies directly in every member state. Its core is a blacklist of ten designs that would be unfair in all circumstances, so authorities would no longer have to prove harm case by case. According to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/09/eu-to-blacklist-ten-manipulative-online-design-traps-leaked-draft-shows), the list includes making it harder to cancel than to sign up, burying prices or renewal terms, false claims that stock is low or a product is popular, pre-ticked add-ons, framing "no" as the wrong choice, nagging people to change a decision already made, confusing double negatives, and fake notifications. The Commission could add new tricks later without going back to lawmakers.

Other parts of the draft:

- **Prices:** drip pricing, where unavoidable fees appear only at checkout, would be banned. Sellers using dynamic pricing for tickets would have to disclose how many items are available at the advertised price.
- **Subscriptions:** a clear "terminate the contract here" button, reminders before automatic renewal, and no free trial turning into a paid plan without active agreement.
- **Children:** paid loot boxes and pay-to-win features banned for minors in games, in-game currencies shown in real money, and advertising based on profiling minors banned for every trader, not just the large platforms already covered by the Digital Services Act.
- **Attention:** every user could switch off autoplay, unrelated notifications, streak rewards and chatbot features that simulate a relationship to keep people engaged.
- **AI agents:** for the first time in EU consumer law, rules for AI shopping agents. Users would choose which purchases need their approval, and agents would have to disclose sponsored suggestions.
- **Fines** of up to 6% of global annual turnover, and a right to reach a human in customer service.

The rules would apply to any business selling online to people in the EU, wherever it is based. The Commission's impact assessment, as reported by Euronews, estimates that they would spare consumers €88.4 billion in losses between 2025 and 2035, against recurring business costs of about €678 million a year.

## How common are these tricks?

Very common, and not only in Europe. In a [2023 EU screening](https://cyprus.representation.ec.europa.eu/news/consumer-protection-manipulative-online-practices-found-148-out-399-online-shops-screened-2023-01-30_en), authorities found manipulative practices on 148 of 399 online shops, including fake countdown timers on 42 of them. A Commission study from 2022, cited by the consumer group [BEUC](https://www.beuc.eu/press-releases/eus-plan-tackle-manipulative-digital-practices-will-benefit-consumers), found at least one dark pattern on 97% of the most popular websites and apps used in the EU.

The widest evidence comes from a [2024 sweep](https://www.icpen.org/news/1360) by 27 consumer authorities in 26 countries, coordinated by the international network ICPEN. Of 642 traders selling subscriptions, 75.7% used at least one dark pattern and 66.8% used two or more. Among subscriptions that renew automatically, [81%](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/232623/Public-Report-ICPEN-Dark-Patterns-Sweep.pdf) gave no way to turn off renewal during purchase, and 70% gave no information on how to cancel. A sweep identifies "possible" dark patterns; it does not decide which are illegal under each country's law.

## Others got there first, with mixed results

Europe is not the first to try. **India** published [Guidelines for the Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/consumer-protection/ccpa-issues-guidelines-for-prevention-and-regulation-of-dark-patterns-2023-effective-30-november-2023) in November 2023, naming 13 practices, among them false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm-shaming, subscription traps and drip pricing. The list is strikingly close to the EU draft. Enforcement has been modest: according to [Bar & Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/law-firms/view-point/from-nudge-to-notice-what-indias-dark-pattern-orders-mean-for-digital-business), after platforms were told to audit themselves in 2025, the regulator penalised seven platforms and collected about 2 million rupees in total, a few tens of thousands of dollars.

The **United States** took a different route. The Federal Trade Commission's "click-to-cancel" rule was [struck down](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2025/07/us-court-cancels-ftc-rule-that-would-have-made-canceling-subscriptions-easier/) by a federal appeals court in July 2025, days before it took effect, because the agency had skipped a required economic analysis, not because the court found easy cancellation unreasonable. Yet a case under existing law produced one of the largest results so far: in September 2025 [Amazon agreed to pay $2.5 billion](https://apnews.com/article/amazon-prime-ftc-bezos-online-shopping-a3aa849de1279e3675a162ec6815de84), including $1.5 billion in refunds, over allegations that it pushed people into Prime and made cancelling hard. That supports both sides of the European debate: existing law can bite, but only when a well-resourced enforcer is willing to litigate for years.

## The case against

Business groups do not defend fake timers, but they make three serious arguments. First, duplication: [CCIA Europe](https://ccianet.org/news/2026/10/upcoming-digital-fairness-act-risks-duplicating-existing-eu-rules-new-explainer-warns/) says most of these practices are already illegal under existing EU rules, and the real problem is weak enforcement, so a new law adds a layer without fixing it. Second, timing and cost: a [joint statement](https://www.digitaleurope.org/news/joint-statement-on-digital-fairness-act-resetting-the-trajectory-for-effective-consumer-protection-in-line-with-simplification-and-better-regulation-principles/) led by DIGITALEUROPE says the law contradicts the EU's own promise to simplify rules and help competitiveness, and that it comes before the Digital Services Act, Digital Markets Act and AI Act are fully applied.

The third argument is the subtlest. [IAB Europe](https://iabeurope.eu/the-european-digital-advertising-industry-calls-for-a-proportionate-digital-fairness-act/), representing advertisers, warns that a broad idea of "dynamic vulnerability" could push companies to collect *more* personal data, in order to work out who is vulnerable at a given moment. A rule meant to protect people could end up profiling them more. It also argues that free, ad-funded services depend on personalisation.

There is also a geopolitical layer. Washington has repeatedly tied trade concessions to Europe's digital rules: in October [POLITICO](https://www.politico.eu/article/us-renews-push-for-eu-to-soften-big-tech-rules/) reported that the US had again linked lower steel tariffs to softer enforcement of the Digital Markets Act, and the US ambassador asked whether the EU was trying "to generate revenue from American companies". The DFA covers every online seller, European and Chinese included, but a law with 6% fines will be read in that light.

## Do rules on paper change the screen?

The most sobering evidence comes from the EU's own past. The GDPR required that refusing cookies be as easy as accepting them. A [2020 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02479) of consent pop-ups on the 10,000 most popular UK websites found that only 11.8% met even minimal legal requirements. The law existed; the design did not change, because enforcement was slow and fragmented across national authorities.

That is why the companion proposal matters as much as the blacklist. BEUC notes that the DFA comes with an update of the rules on cross-border enforcement that would give the Commission more powers. A blacklist helps here too: an authority only has to show that a practice is on the list, not that it harmed a particular consumer.

## Opinion: ban the clear cases, then fund the enforcers

The core of the draft is right. Nobody needs a fake countdown timer, a pre-ticked insurance box or a cancel button hidden in a maze, and treating them as illegal everywhere is simpler for honest businesses too, which no longer compete with rivals who cheat. The children's rules are the strongest part. A paid loot box works much like a slot machine with cartoon graphics, and a child cannot weigh it as an adult can.

The industry's best point deserves to be taken seriously: much of this was arguably already illegal under the 2005 directive, and the cookie-banner story shows what happens when a good rule meets weak enforcement. The test of the DFA will not be the length of its list but whether someone checks the screens. Parliament should also tighten "vulnerability" so that protecting people never becomes a reason to profile them further. If the law ends up as another banner nobody reads, Brussels will have proved its critics right.
