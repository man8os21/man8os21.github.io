On 17 November, oil started moving again through a pipe that had been dead since 2014. Iraq's oil minister, Basim Mohammed Khudair, said pumping had resumed through "the old Iraq-Turkey pipeline" at 120,000 barrels a day, with a plan to raise it gradually to 500,000, [according to Rudaw](https://rudaw.net/english/categories/economic/1080192). The same day the cabinet approved a wider rehabilitation of the line, [Anadolu reported](https://aa.com.tr/en/middle-east/iraq-approves-rehabilitation-of-oil-pipeline-to-turkiye-as-flows-resume-after-12-years/4128355).

Most headlines framed it the same way: a way around the Strait of Hormuz. That is partly true. But the numbers, and Iraq's internal politics, tell a more complicated story.

## What exactly reopened

There is not one "Iraq–Turkey pipeline" but a system. Its Turkish half runs to the Mediterranean port of Ceyhan. Its Iraqi half used to start in Kirkuk and Baiji and run north through Nineveh province to Fishkhabour, on the border. That Iraqi section went out of service in March 2014 after repeated attacks, including by ISIS, wrecked long stretches of it, [Shafaq News explains](https://shafaq.com/en/Economy/Kirkuk-Turkiye-oil-pipeline-restarts-after-12-year-shutdown). The section that has now restarted is this federal line. Capacity figures vary by source and by what is being measured: Rudaw puts the federal line at up to a million barrels a day, Al Jazeera puts the whole Iraq–Turkey system at about 1.5 million, and the current transit deal covers 750,000. Before 2014 the route carried roughly 0.5% of global oil supply, [Arab News notes](https://www.arabnews.com/middle-east/iraq-resumes-oil-exports-through-turkish-pipeline-after-12-year-break-3012601).

After 2014, oil kept reaching Ceyhan by a different road: a pipeline built by the Kurdistan Regional Government (KRG), which crosses the Kurdish region and joins the Turkish system at Fishkhabour. Before the restart, that Kurdish route was carrying only about 170,000 barrels a day, according to Turkish data cited by Al Jazeera in August. So the new restart does not create Iraq's only northern route. It creates a second one, and crucially, one that Baghdad controls without Erbil.

## Why oil stopped flowing north at all

The northern route has been closed more often than open. In March 2023 an arbitration panel at the International Chamber of Commerce ruled that Turkey owed Iraq about $1.5 billion for letting Kurdish crude be exported without Baghdad's consent between 2014 and 2018, and Turkey stopped the flows, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/iraq-halts-northern-crude-exports-after-winning-arbitration-case-against-turkey-2023-03-25/). Kurdish exports only restarted in September 2025, after a three-way deal between Baghdad, Erbil and the oil companies, [according to AP](https://apnews.com/article/iraq-oil-exports-resume-kurdistan-263c440d9a320949ebfe5d437087c8d5).

Then the legal basis itself lapsed. Turkey announced in 2025 that the 1973 pipeline treaty would end on 27 July 2026 ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/turkey-says-pipeline-agreement-with-iraq-expire-july-2026-2025-07-21/)). On 1 August the two state oil sides signed a one-year transit arrangement covering 750,000 barrels a day while a long-term deal is negotiated, [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/1/turkiye-and-iraq-sign-one-year-oil-pipeline-deal-amid-global-shift).

## Why Hormuz made it urgent

Iraq is a country that lives on oil and ships almost all of it by sea from Basra. More than 90% of its budget comes from oil sales, [The National notes](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/energy/2026/11/17/iraq-resumes-pumping-crude-oil-for-export-through-pipeline-to-turkey/). When the US–Israeli war with Iran began in late February and tankers stopped crossing the strait, exports fell from 3.36 million barrels a day in February to 131,000 in April, the same paper reports. Output at the southern fields fell by about 80% as storage tanks filled, [Reuters reported in March](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/iraq-oil-output-further-plunges-storage-fills-hormuz-exports-blocked-by-conflict-2026-03-25/).

For Iraq this is not a market problem but a payroll problem. In the first nine months of 2026, the state spent 46.13 trillion dinars on employee compensation while oil brought in about 38.5 trillion, and the deficit grew by 346% year on year, according to finance ministry data reported by [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/business/iraqs-budget-deficit-surges-346-as-oil-revenues-slump-3009952). The same report cites a World Bank forecast that Iraq's economy will shrink by 12.4% this year. The draft 2027 budget sets aside about $76.3 billion for salaries alone ([Rudaw](https://www.rudaw.net/english/categories/analysis/1079130)).

## How much can one pipeline really do?

Here the honest answer is: not much, compared with the sea. Before the war, Iraq exported around 3.7 million barrels a day, according to its oil ministry as quoted by Rudaw. The new line starts at 120,000 and aims for 500,000, with no timetable. Even with the Kurdish line added, and with the roughly 120,000–150,000 barrels a day that Iraq now sends by about 6,000 tanker trucks to Syria's port of Baniyas (Rudaw), northern and overland routes cover a fraction of what Basra ships.

The wider Gulf has the same problem. The US Energy Information Administration estimated that only about 2.6 million barrels a day of spare capacity in Saudi and Emirati bypass pipelines could be used if Hormuz closed, against around 20 million barrels a day that normally cross the strait ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinEnergy/detail.php?id=65504)).

And the strait has partly reopened. Iraq's production reached 4.6 million barrels a day in October, the highest since 2019, as shuttle tankers moved more crude out of the Gulf, according to the IEA data reported by [OilPrice.com](https://oilprice.com/Latest-Energy-News/World-News/Iraqs-Oil-Production-Soars-to-2019-High-as-Hormuz-Flows-Surge.html). Kpler data cited by [Rudaw](https://www.rudaw.net/english/categories/middleeast/1080185) put Middle East crude exports at about 90% of pre-war volumes in November, with roughly 60% still moving through Hormuz.

So the pipeline is not a replacement. It is insurance: a second door that stays open when the main one is shut. The minister also pointed to a quieter benefit: oil sold through the northern outlet, he said, can be marketed "without price discounts" (Rudaw).

## A pipeline is a target too

Pipelines are not safe just because they are on land. This one was closed for 12 years precisely because armed groups kept attacking it. In March, drones hit the Sarsang field in the Kurdish region and halted its output; Kurdish officials blamed Iran-aligned Iraqi militias, while no group claimed the attack at first, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/oilfield-iraqi-kurdistan-halts-production-after-drone-strike-sources-say-2026-03-05/). A route through northern Iraq trades one chokepoint, a strait Iran can threaten, for hundreds of kilometres of exposed steel that militias can reach.

It also changes who the customers are. In 2024, China took 34.4% of Iraq's seaborne crude, India 27.1% and the EU 17.4%, according to shipbroker Banchero Costa data published by the [World Ports Organization](https://www.worldports.org/tanker-market-iraq-crude-oil-exports-down-so-far-in-2025/). Ceyhan sits on the Mediterranean, far closer to European refineries than to Asian ones. More oil going north does little for Asian buyers, the biggest customers of Basra, who still depend on Hormuz.

## Who wins and who loses inside Iraq

The federal line matters as much for Baghdad–Erbil politics as for Hormuz. In March, Iraq's then oil minister, Hayyan Abdul-Ghani, said Kirkuk oil would soon flow "without passing through the Kurdistan Region", while the KRG accused Baghdad of a "suffocating embargo" ([Rudaw](https://www.rudaw.net/english/categories/economic/942849)). A day later the two sides agreed to send Kirkuk crude through the Kurdish pipeline with all revenue to the federal treasury ([KRG statement](https://gov.krd/english/news-and-announcements/posts/2026/march/statement-by-the-krg-negotiating-team-regarding-oil-exports/)).

Now Baghdad no longer needs that favour. Its motive is not only money: it insists that Kirkuk's oil, in a province both Baghdad and Erbil claim, is sold by the federal state. For the KRG, whose pipeline was one of its few bargaining chips, the restart is a loss, and its officials argue that Baghdad has failed to stop militia attacks on the region's energy infrastructure while squeezing its budget (Rudaw, March). Kurdish officials are already complaining that the planned "Development Road" corridor to Turkey largely bypasses Kurdish areas ([Kurdistan24](https://www.kurdistan24.net/en/story/948645)).

## What Turkey wants

Ankara wants Ceyhan to become a hub. Energy minister Alparslan Bayraktar has talked of extending the line south to Basra and of a corridor that could carry 2.5 million barrels a day and possibly Qatari gas to Europe ([Türkiye Today](https://www.turkiyetoday.com/business/turkiye-eyes-to-turn-iraq-trade-route-into-80b-energy-corridor-minister-says-3229691)). Iraq's prime minister has spoken of supplying Turkey with up to a million barrels a day ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/18/iraq-pumps-oil-to-turkey-again-after-12-years-to-bypass-hormuz-disruption)).

For Iraq, that is both an opportunity and a new dependence. Turkey controls the upstream dams on the Tigris and Euphrates, and in November 2025 the two countries agreed that money from Iraqi oil sold to Turkey would pay Turkish companies for Iraqi water projects, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/iraq-turkey-sign-deal-iraqi-water-infrastructure-projects-2025-11-02/). Oil, water and transit are becoming one bargain, and Ankara holds more of the cards. Turkish officials would answer that the pipeline only works if both sides gain from it, and that a hub at Ceyhan gives Gulf producers an exit Iran cannot close.

## Opinion: a good insurance policy, not an escape

The restart is sensible. A country that pays its public sector out of oil money and ships nearly all of it through one strait should own a second exit, and Iraq has paid heavily this year for not having one. But the pipeline does not end Iraq's dependence on Hormuz. At 500,000 barrels a day, if it ever gets there, it would carry well under a sixth of Iraq's pre-war exports.

What it really changes is power. It gives Baghdad an alternative to the Kurdish pipeline, which is fair as a matter of sovereignty but dangerous if it is used to starve Erbil rather than to share revenue by agreement. And it gives Turkey more say over Iraq's income, on top of its say over Iraq's water. The best outcome is boring: both northern lines running, revenue shared under rules both Iraqi sides accept, and a long-term transit treaty with Turkey signed before the one-year deal runs out next summer. The worst is a pipeline that becomes one more lever in fights Iraq cannot afford.
