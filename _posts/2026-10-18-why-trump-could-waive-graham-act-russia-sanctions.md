Saturday, 18 October 2026, was supposed to be the day a new American law started to bite Russia. The Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act set tariffs of up to 500% on Russian goods, 100% on goods from the biggest buyers of Russian oil and gas, and mandatory sanctions on Russian banks, officials and shadow-fleet tankers. Two days earlier, on the evening of 16 October, the president signed an [executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/10/implementing-the-lindsey-o-graham-sanctioning-russia-and-iran-act-of-2026/) that set those rates on paper and then waived almost all of them.

The obvious question: if Congress passed it and the president signed it, how can one order switch it off? The short answer is that Congress wrote the off switch into the law itself.

## The switch is in section 115

The Graham Act became law on 18 September 2026. Its [section 115](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/10815) says the president "may" waive any sanction, restriction or duty under the Act. There is one condition. Before the waiver, he must send Congress a written certification that it is "in the national interests of the United States" and a report explaining why. The report must be unclassified, though it may carry a classified annex.

That is all. The section sets no waiting period, vote or time limit. The order says the certification and report were sent. According to a [sanctions law firm's analysis](https://fesenkolaw.com/blog/graham-act-waiver-what-trumps-october-16-2026-order-suspends/), neither has been made public.

The waiver also has no end date. Under the order, it lasts until the president, or the Secretary of State after consulting the Treasury and the US Trade Representative, decides that "further action" is appropriate.

## Waiving is not the same as lifting

The law draws a sharp line between a waiver and a termination. [Section 117](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ111/html/PLAW-119publ111.htm) covers ending sanctions for good. For Russia, the president must first certify that Russia has signed a peace agreement accepted by Ukraine's government and stopped all hostilities. Then the termination cannot take effect for 30 days, and during that time Congress can pass a joint resolution of disapproval to block it.

A waiver skips all of that. So a law that looks hard to undo on the termination side is easy to park on the waiver side.

Waivers themselves are common in sanctions laws, because Congress usually wants to leave the president room in diplomacy. What stands out here is the scale: almost the whole Act, two days before it started.

## What is suspended, and what is not

The order waives the tariffs, the mandatory blocking of persons under section 102, the bank measures in section 103, and the new restrictions on Americans in sections 105 to 111. It also waives section 102(d), which had turned existing executive-order sanctions into statute, and section 117 along with it.

What it does **not** do is lift any existing sanction. Section 4(b) of the order keeps in force everything imposed under the executive orders of the Russia national emergency, including every person already designated. As the [FesenkoLaw analysis](https://fesenkolaw.com/blog/graham-act-waiver-what-trumps-october-16-2026-order-suspends/) points out, Sberbank, VTB and Gazprombank were already blocked under Executive Order 14024, and new American investment in Russia was already banned under a 2022 order. Much of the Act restated prohibitions that were already there.

So the honest summary is this: no new pressure arrives, and the old pressure stays exactly where it was. Russia gets no relief from existing sanctions, but it is spared the large new escalation that was due on 18 October, above all the threat of tariffs on its oil customers.

## Why the president says he did it

The official reason is the national interest. Secretary of State Marco Rubio told reporters that the president wants 60 days "to analyze their impact on the U.S. and global economy given the pressure on global energy supplies," [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/trump-administration/trump-suspends-new-russia-sanctions-deal-purchase-diesel-citing-nation-rcna603991). The same report says diesel prices have risen almost 70% since the war with Iran began in February, that the waiver followed a deal announced the week before to release Russian diesel onto the world market, and that the midterm elections are less than three weeks away.

Critics in both parties disagree. Ukraine's president called it "a financial reward for Putin". Republican Senator Thom Tillis noted that the Senate had passed the Act 86 to 11.

## A stronger brake existed in 2017

Congress knows how to build a tighter lock, because it did so before. The 2017 sanctions law known as CAATSA, in [section 216](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/9511), requires a report and a 30-day review not only before terminating Russia sanctions, but also before **waiving** them for a sanctioned person. During that review the president may not act, and Congress can pass a resolution of disapproval.

Even that brake has limits, as January 2019 showed. The Treasury planned to lift sanctions on Rusal and other companies linked to Oleg Deripaska. A disapproval resolution won 57 votes to 42 in the Senate, with 11 Republicans joining Democrats, but that fell short of the 60 votes needed to end debate, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-usa-russia-sanctions/bid-to-keep-u-s-sanctions-on-russias-rusal-fails-in-senate-idUSKCN1PA2JB/). And Reuters noted that to actually stop the president, the resolution would have needed two-thirds in both chambers to override a veto.

The lesson is that review procedures buy time and attention. They do not, by themselves, beat a determined president unless Congress has veto-proof numbers.

## Can Congress undo it now?

Not through the Graham Act. Since section 115 has no review, Congress can reverse the waiver only by passing a new law, and a president can veto a law aimed at his own decision. Overriding a veto needs two-thirds of both chambers.

The Senate vote of 86 to 11 was above that line. The House was not. It approved the Act [262 to 159](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/2026308?BillNum=H.R.5334), about 62% of those voting, and 152 Democrats voted no; many Democrats opposed the bill because it gave the president new tariff powers, according to NBC. A bill that only blocks the waiver might draw different votes, but the starting arithmetic is short of two-thirds. Timing also matters: the House and Senate are on recess until after Election Day, [AP reported](https://apnews.com/article/trump-sanctions-waiver-russa-putin-ukraine-war-3cc9740c39e9e9eb713b1c553b5709e6).

## Four ways this could go

**1. Partial return after 60 days.** If the stated review is real, the administration could restore some pieces, such as sanctions on shadow-fleet tankers, while keeping the tariffs on oil buyers waived. That would match the energy-price reason given.

**2. Quiet permanence.** The waiver has no end date, so it can simply stay. The Act runs until 2031 but sits dormant, and the existing executive-order sanctions do the real work.

**3. Sudden reversal.** The order already sets the tariff rates and delegates the work, so revoking the waiver takes one decision. If talks with Moscow fail or energy prices ease, the full package could return quickly, which is why banks and traders cannot treat this as permission.

**4. Congress legislates.** After the election, members could pass a narrower law, for example one blocking purchases of Russian oil. Without two-thirds in the House it would likely face a veto it cannot override, unless the election changes the numbers or the White House agrees to a compromise.

## My view

*This section is opinion.*

The waiver is legal; the law plainly allows it. But legal and wise are different questions. Congress spent a year and a half building pressure and then wrote a waiver that asks for nothing more than a report nobody outside government can read. That was Congress's own choice, and it should fix it: the CAATSA model of a short review before any broad waiver is a reasonable standard, whoever is president.

On the substance, I think waiving almost everything at once, rather than only the tariffs that bear on energy prices, weakens the message to Moscow more than it lowers prices at the pump. The test is simple and checkable: if the 60-day review ends with nothing restored, the "review" was a delay in name only.
