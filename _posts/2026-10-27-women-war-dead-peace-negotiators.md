On 26 October 2026 the UN Secretary-General published his annual report on women, peace and security. Two of its numbers sit badly next to each other. In 2025 the UN documented more than 37,000 civilians killed in 20 armed conflicts, and [one in four was a woman or a girl](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/10/1168673), double the share of 2021. In the same year, across 57 peace processes, women were [just 5% of the negotiators](https://reliefweb.int/report/world/report-secretary-general-women-and-peace-and-security-s2026758-enarruzh), down from 7% the year before.

## Why more women are dying

Men are still more likely to be killed in war. What has changed is how wars are fought. According to [UN Women](https://www.unwomen.org/en/articles/facts-and-figures/facts-and-figures-women-peace-and-security), the use of long-range explosive weapons in crowded places, homes, schools, clinics, markets and displacement camps, pushes up the share of women and girls among the dead. When the front line is a residential block, the people at home are the ones hit.

The same report counts 9,788 verified cases of conflict-related sexual violence in 2025, more than double the year before, and an estimated 756 million women and girls living within 50 kilometres of a deadly conflict event, the highest since the 1990s.

## Why women are missing from the table

The simplest explanation is also the oldest: peace talks are built around the people who can stop the shooting. Mediators invite the armed parties, and armed parties send their commanders and politicians, who are mostly men. Women who organised shelters, ran local ceasefires or documented abuse are usually treated as victims or civil society, not as parties. A 2015 [International Peace Institute report](https://www.ipinst.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/IPI-E-pub-Reimagining-Peacemaking.pdf) by Marie O'Reilly, Andrea Ó Súilleabháin and Thania Paffenholz argued that this is a design choice made by mediators and parties, not a law of nature.

A second reason is who runs the talks today. The UN's own [infographic](https://reliefweb.int/attachments/969826e9-c454-5fe7-99a9-9496f5cc23b6/Infographic-Report-of-the-UN-secretary-general-on-women-peace-and-security-2026-EN.pdf) notes that the number of processes the UN leads or co-leads has shrunk from 14 fifteen years ago to three in 2025 (Cyprus, the Geneva talks on Georgia, and Libya). In those three, women were 22% of negotiators, more than four times the global average. Most mediation now runs through individual states and regional bodies, from Gulf monarchies and Turkey to the United States and the African Union, and each sets its own expectations about who sits at the table. Women were also only 14% of mediators on average, so the gap is not only on the side of the warring parties.

The report also found that four out of five peace processes had no women negotiators at all, and that none of the 13 ceasefire agreements signed in 2025 named sexual violence as a prohibited act.

## Does it matter? What the evidence says

Here the honest answer is: probably yes, but less certainly than the slogans claim.

The figure quoted most often is that a peace agreement is 35% more likely to last 15 years when women take part. It comes from a statistical analysis by Laurel Stone of 182 agreements signed between 1989 and 2011, published as an annex to a [2015 policy report](https://www.ipinst.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/IPI-E-pub-Reimagining-Peacemaking.pdf), not in a peer-reviewed journal.

The strongest peer-reviewed study is by [Jana Krause, Werner Krause and Piia Bränfors](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03050629.2018.1492386) (2018). Looking at civil-war peace agreements, they found a robust correlation between agreements signed by women and longer peace, after controlling for factors such as democracy, income, power sharing and UN peacekeeping. Agreements signed by women also contained more political reform provisions and had higher implementation rates. Their explanation is practical rather than romantic. In Guatemala in the early 1990s, for example, Luz Méndez, the only woman in the rebel URNG delegation, worked with women's groups that had no seat of their own, and the accords ended up with unusually strong gender-equality commitments. More generally, women delegates tended to work with women's civil-society groups, which pushed for better content and kept pressure on implementation. They also found how rare this is: of 130 agreements between 1990 and 2014, women signed only 13.

A 2026 article in [International Affairs](https://doi.org/10.1093/ia/iiag045) by Lenka Olejníková, Laura J. Shepherd and Alba Rosa Boer Cueva adds a needed caution. Reviewing UN and national policy documents, they found the durability claim rests on essentially three studies, often cited in circles. In Stone's working paper, they write, the estimated effect is "remarkably unstable" across statistical models. Their conclusion is that the evidence is promising but tentative, a correlation rather than proof of cause.

There is a plausible alternative explanation, too: processes that include women may already be the ones where the parties are more serious about peace. That would make women's presence partly a sign of a good process rather than its cause, and no study so far has fully ruled this out.

A common worry among practitioners is the opposite one: that every extra voice slows talks down and gives spoilers a platform. The limited evidence does not support that fear for women's groups specifically. Paffenholz's study of 40 peace processes in 35 countries, as summarised by [Inclusive Security](https://www.inclusivesecurity.org/publication/inclusive-security-and-peaceful-societies-exploring-the-evidence/), found that when women's groups had real influence, an agreement was reached in all but one case. That study, too, was published as policy research rather than in a peer-reviewed journal, so it carries the same caveat.

## What participation looks like in practice

Cases help more than averages. In Colombia, [Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/womens-participation-peace-processes-colombia), only one of the twenty negotiators was a woman when talks opened in 2012. By 2015, according to the [Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/womens-participation-peace-processes-colombia), women were 20% of the government team and 43% of the FARC delegation, a share CFR notes was consistent with the share of women among the guerrilla's fighters. Many of these women were combatants, not peacemakers by nature. A first-ever gender sub-commission helped write provisions on women's land rights, political participation and sexual violence. Yet the [Kroc Institute](https://kroc.nd.edu/news-events/news/kroc-institute-releases-special-report-on-implementation-status-of-gender-approach-within-colombian-peace-agreement/), which monitors the deal, tracks 130 gender commitments and found that by November 2022 only 12% were complete and 52% were at a minimal level. Words on paper are not implementation.

In the Philippines, [Miriam Coronel-Ferrer](https://www.unwomen.org/en/news/stories/2020/10/in-the-words-of-miriam-coronel-ferrer) became the first woman chief negotiator to sign a final peace accord with a rebel group, the 2014 agreement with the Moro Islamic Liberation Front. In Liberia, Leymah Gbowee organised women across ethnic and religious lines to push for an end to the civil war, work recognised in the [2011 Nobel Peace Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2011/press-release/), which she shared with Ellen Johnson Sirleaf and Yemen's Tawakkol Karman. In Africa, the African Union created [FemWise-Africa](https://peaceau.org/en/article/femwise-africa-convenes-first-general-assembly-and-launches-accreditation-process-for-500-african-women-mediators-in-constantine-algeria), a network of women mediators, in 2017.

## The objections, taken seriously

Not every government sees this agenda the same way. At the Security Council's October 2025 debate, Russia's mission [argued](https://russiaun.ru/en/news/306102025) that women's participation cannot be achieved "by merely imposing artificial quotas on countries affected by conflict" or by bringing in women from developed countries or foreign-funded NGOs "for the sake of appearances". It said real progress needs economic advancement, education and skilled work for women, that local conditions must be respected, and it objected to what it called "neoliberal concepts" around gender in UN documents.

Some of that is politics. But part of it echoes concerns heard in the Global South: that participation decided in donor capitals can produce tokens, not influence. And resistance has not come from one side only. In 2019, according to [Security Council Report](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/whatsinblue/2019/05/in-hindsight-negotiations-on-resolution-2467-on-sexual-violence-in-conflict.php), a resolution on sexual violence in conflict lost its language on sexual and reproductive health because of a US veto threat, while China and Russia abstained over other parts of the text.

Mediators also face a real constraint. Their first job is to stop the killing, and armed parties choose their own delegations. Many governments and rebel movements see outside demands about who they send as interference in their own affairs. A mediator who insists on quotas may lose the parties altogether, and a delegation that adds women only to satisfy donors may give them no real say.

## Opinion: count the dead and count the chairs

*This section is the author's opinion.*

I am not persuaded by the argument that women make better peace because they are gentler. That idea flatters no one and the evidence does not need it. The stronger argument is simpler. When a quarter of the civilian dead are women and only one process in five has any women negotiators, the people deciding the terms of peace are working with half the picture. In many wars it is women who run shelters, search for the missing and keep local services going, and that knowledge belongs in the room. A ceasefire that does not forbid rape, as none of last year's 13 did, has left something out.

The evidence that women's presence makes peace last longer is real but not yet strong, and advocates should say so. Overselling a fragile statistic gives opponents an easy target.

Quotas imposed from abroad are not the answer the critics fear, and they do not have to be. In the cases above, Guatemala, Colombia, the Philippines and Liberia, the push came largely from women inside those countries, and the critics are right that this is what lasts. Colombia also shows the limit: getting women into the room did not guarantee that the promises were kept. The practical step is for mediators, whoever they are, to ask the parties for women delegates, give local women's groups a formal channel into the talks, and write sexual violence into every ceasefire as a violation. None of that requires a quota, and none of it should be the price that stops a ceasefire. It is a request, made every time, until it becomes normal.
