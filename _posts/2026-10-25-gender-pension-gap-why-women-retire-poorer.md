On Saturday 24 October, between 25,000 people (the government's count) and 50,000 (the organisers') marched through Madrid to defend Spain's public pensions, [according to El País](https://elpais.com/economia/2026-10-24/miles-de-personas-se-manifiestan-en-madrid-en-defensa-del-sistema-de-pensiones-no-se-venden.html). One demand came with two numbers: men's average pension of 1,638 euros a month against women's 1,139. That is almost 500 euros less, every month, for the rest of a woman's life.

Spain is not unusual. The gap between retired men and retired women is one of the most stable features of pension systems almost everywhere.

## How big is it?

The OECD, the club of mostly rich economies, measures the gap among pension recipients aged 65 and over. In [Pensions at a Glance 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_e40274c1-en/full-report/gender-pension-gap_90ed13b5.html), women's average pensions were 23% lower than men's in 2024: about 77 cents for every dollar or euro a man receives. In 2007 the gap was 28%, so it is shrinking, slowly.

The spread is huge: under 10% in Czechia, Estonia, Iceland, Slovakia and Slovenia, above 35% in Austria, Mexico, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, and 47% in Japan. [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/en/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20260225-1) puts the EU figure at 24.5%, largest in Malta (40.3%) and smallest in Estonia (5.6%).

## Where does it come from?

Pensions mirror working lives. Most systems pay more to people who earned more and contributed longer, so every gap in paid work returns decades later as a gap in retirement income.

The OECD estimates women's expected lifetime earnings at 35% below men's, with three roughly equal causes:

- **Shorter careers.** Women's expected working life is about 34 years, almost six shorter than men's (the difference was 18 years in 1980).
- **Fewer hours.** Employed women work about 5.1 fewer hours a week.
- **Lower hourly pay.** The hourly wage gap is about 11%.

Much of this traces back to unpaid care. The International Labour Organization [estimated in 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-calls-urgent-action-prevent-looming-global-care-crisis) that women do 76.2% of the world's unpaid care work, about 4 hours 25 minutes a day against 1 hour 23 minutes for men. But care is not the whole story: the OECD also points to lasting differences in education and career paths, which reflect a mix of constraint and choice that economists still argue about.

## In much of the world, the gap is having no pension at all

The OECD and Eurostat compare people who already receive a pension. That fits Europe, where nearly everyone gets something, far better than much of Africa, South Asia or the Arab world.

The ILO's [World Social Protection Report](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/wcmsp5/groups/public/%40dgreports/%40dcomm/%40publ/documents/publication/wcms_821426.pdf) found that 38.7% of working-age men worldwide were actively contributing to a pension scheme, against 26.3% of women. In the Arab States only 13.7% of women were even legally covered by contributory schemes. Women are over-represented in family farm work, domestic work and informal self-employment, which pension law often does not reach.

Pensions that do not depend on contributions change that picture. South Africa's means-tested [Older Persons Grant](https://www.gov.za/services/services-residents/social-benefits/old-age-pension) is paid from age 60, and in one [rural study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4730476/) 81.6% of eligible women reported receiving it, against 70.1% of eligible men.

## Why the headline number can mislead

The OECD itself warns that the pension gap does not measure how well older women live. It ignores income sharing within households and leaves out people with no pension. Across OECD countries there is no correlation between the pension gap and the gap in disposable income among people over 66, which averages about 10%.

Malta shows how odd the indicator can be. A [Central Bank of Malta paper](https://www.centralbankmalta.org/site/Publications/Economic%20Research/2026/DP-3-26.pdf) notes that its gap rose from 31.5% in 2015 to 40.3% in 2024 even as the government raised minimum pensions and credited parents who stopped work. The authors argue this was largely a statistical quirk: the reforms brought many women with small pensions into the count, while a woman with no pension does not count at all.

There is also a men's side. Women can expect 22.8 years after leaving the labour market, men 18.6, according to the OECD. Over a lifetime, total pension money is less unequal than the monthly figure, although a smaller pension stretched over more years is still smaller each month.

## What governments are trying

**Care credits.** Many systems count child-rearing years as contribution years. The OECD finds mandatory pensions cushion about half the loss from a five-year child-related break. Credits cost money, though, in systems already strained by ageing.

**Spain's top-up.** Spain once paid a supplement only to mothers. In 2019 the EU Court of Justice ruled in [case C-450/18](https://curia.europa.eu/site/upload/docs/application/pdf/2019-12/cp190154en.pdf) that this discriminated against fathers in the same situation. The replacement [gender-gap supplement](https://www.seg-social.es/wps/portal/wss/internet/Trabajadores/PrestacionesPensionesTrabajadores/4c43ce49-6636-4a12-bacf-5e6697eb81da) pays 36.90 euros a month per child in 2026, up to four children; fathers can qualify under stricter conditions, and a parent convicted of violence against the mother or children cannot.

**Equal retirement ages.** The OECD urges an end to earlier retirement for women. China is moving that way: a [2024 decision](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/kgfb/202409/t20240913_439534.html) raises women's statutory ages from 50 to 55 and from 55 to 58 over 15 years from January 2025, and men's from 60 to 63, with an option to retire up to three years earlier once minimum contributions are met. Beijing's motive is a rapidly ageing population, and the [BBC notes](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c62421le4j6o) a 2019 state think-tank forecast that the main pension fund could run dry by 2035. Many workers [are unhappy](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/politics/article/3279155/no-one-happy-chinas-move-lift-retirement-age-unpopular-among-workers).

**Japan's homemaker rule.** Japan's "Category 3" lets spouses of company employees earning under 1.3 million yen a year get a basic pension without paying premiums. [The Mainichi](https://mainichi.jp/english/articles/20260414/p2a/00m/0na/008000c) reports it was created about 40 years ago to give homemakers pension rights in their own name; critics say it now discourages part-timers from earning more. The governing parties agreed in April 2026 to discuss scaling it back, while lawmakers said that would need national debate.

**Chile's longevity fix.** Chile's 2025 reform, [Law 21.735](https://www.bcn.cl/leychile/navegar?idNorma=1212060), adds a monthly payment so that eligible women with the same savings and retirement age as a man get the same pension, even though annuity tables assume they will live longer.

## Opinion: what I think

I think most of the gap is a bill for care that society received for free and is now charging to the women who gave it. Not all of it, and the choices women make matter too, but the 76% figure is not a coincidence.

The objections to the usual fixes still have a point. Raising women's retirement age is fair on paper and harsh on a 54-year-old who has spent decades on a factory floor. Credits paid only to mothers lock in the idea that care is women's job, which is why the EU court was right to count caring fathers. And chasing a single indicator can mislead, as Malta shows.

The fixes with the best evidence are the least glamorous: basic pensions that reach women outside formal jobs, credits for whoever actually does the caring, and childcare that lets women keep working if they want to. The gap was built over a lifetime. It will be closed over one too, or not at all.
