Israel voted on 27 October 2026, and the morning after, the arithmetic was stark. With about 93% of ordinary ballots counted, the [Central Elections Committee figures](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/with-over-85-of-votes-counted-pro-netanyahu-bloc-gets-53-seats-eisenkot-led-bloc-at-48-and-arab-parties-have-19/) gave Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats, the opposition bloc led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot 48, and two parties backed mostly by Arab citizens, the Joint List and Ra'am, 19 between them. A majority in the 120-seat Knesset is 61. Some 600,000 "double envelope" votes from soldiers, diplomats and others were still to be counted, so seats may move, though analysts expect the balance of power to hold.

The [previous high was 15, in 2020](https://www.timesofisrael.com/as-change-bloc-falls-far-short-of-majority-democrats-golan-urges-coalition-with-arabs/), and the outgoing Knesset had 10. Add those 19 to Eisenkot's 48 and you get 67, a comfortable majority of everyone who wanted Netanyahu gone. Yet almost nobody in Israel treats that as a government. This article explains why.

## How Israel builds a government

Israel elects its parliament by nationwide proportional representation: any list that clears 3.25% of the vote gets seats in proportion to its share. No party has ever won a majority alone, so every government is a coalition. After the results, the president consults each party and asks the candidate most likely to command 61 votes to try. A government does not strictly need 61 members; it needs to win the confidence vote and survive. That is why "outside support", where a party votes for a government without sitting in it, matters so much.

Arab citizens, many of whom call themselves Palestinian citizens of Israel, are about [20% of the population](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/oct/28/israel-election-results-live-updates-exit-polls-netanyahu-likud-eisenkot-latest-news). Their parties range from Hadash (communist, Jewish-Arab) and the Palestinian-nationalist Balad, to Ta'al, to Ra'am, the political arm of the southern Islamic Movement, which is socially conservative and focused on practical gains for Arab towns.

## Three moments when Arab votes mattered

**1992.** Yitzhak Rabin's Labor won 44 seats. According to the [Israel Democracy Institute](https://en.idi.org.il/israeli-elections-and-parties/elections/1992/), Labor, Meretz and the Arab parties together formed a blocking majority of 61, but Rabin "rejected forming a government with the Arab parties" and brought in Shas instead. Hadash and the Arab Democratic Party, five seats in all, [voted for the government from outside](https://www.jta.org/archive/rabin-becomes-prime-minister-with-pledge-to-work-for-peace). When Shas left in 1993, those five votes kept Rabin's minority government alive through the Oslo Accords. His opponents called it a government without a "Jewish majority", a charge that still echoes.

**2015.** A 2014 law raised the threshold from 2% to 3.25%. According to the [Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/sada/2015/03/the-joint-list-that-could), Avigdor Liberman initiated the change, and Carnegie's reading is that he wanted to exclude the small Arab parties. Officially it was part of a "Governance Law" meant to cut the number of parties and stabilise coalitions, as the [Israel Democracy Institute](https://en.idi.org.il/israeli-elections-and-parties/elections/2015/) describes it. Instead, four very different parties merged into the Joint List and became the [third-largest list](https://en.idi.org.il/israeli-elections-and-parties/elections/2015/), with 13 seats. A rule meant to shrink them made them stronger.

**2021.** After four elections in two years, Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas signed a [coalition deal](https://www.timesofisrael.com/arab-israeli-raam-party-makes-history-by-joining-bennett-lapid-coalition/) with Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, the first Arab party in roughly half a century to sit in a governing coalition. Ra'am said it secured more than 53 billion shekels in development plans for Arab towns and the legalisation of three Bedouin villages. The government lasted only a year. In June 2022, a Ra'am lawmaker and a Meretz lawmaker voted against [renewing the law](https://www.timesofisrael.com/two-coalition-mks-deliver-blow-to-colleagues-defeating-west-bank-legal-bill/) that applies Israeli law to settlers in the West Bank, one of several blows that ended it. Outside support, in other words, has worked once (1993 to 1995) and failed once (2022): it holds only as long as the government avoids the votes its partners cannot cast.

The taboo is not only on the Jewish side. In 2021 the Joint List refused to back the Bennett government, and Balad's leader Sami Abou Shehadeh told [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2021/6/5/dangerous-palestinians-on-mansour-abbas-joining-israeli-govt) that Abbas had become "an outcast" for joining a government that would keep the occupation. The Palestinian analyst Diana Buttu said then that "it is not our role to be kingmaker. We are in opposition to this system."

## Why most Jewish parties still say no

Bennett, Liberman and Eisenkot himself all [promised](https://www.timesofisrael.com/as-change-bloc-falls-far-short-of-majority-democrats-golan-urges-coalition-with-arabs/) not to rely on Arab parties. Only Yair Golan's Democrats did not. Their reasons are not all the same, and they deserve to be stated fairly.

- **Security and trust after 7 October 2023.** Israel Democracy Institute president Yohanan Plesner told [JTA](https://www.timesofisrael.com/change-bloc-alliance-with-arabs-unity-government-or-new-election-3-scenarios-for-what-comes-next/) that "tolerance for working with an Islamist party has diminished" since the Hamas attack, and that public opinion is "deeply unprepared" for such a coalition. Ra'am's Abbas condemned the atrocities but has avoided calling Hamas a terror organisation, and one Ra'am lawmaker had to apologise for questioning whether children were killed and women raped that day.
- **Ideology.** Parts of the Joint List reject Israel's definition as a Jewish state. Eisenkot has said he could work with Arab parties only if they recognise it, according to the [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/oct/28/israel-election-results-live-updates-exit-polls-netanyahu-likud-eisenkot-latest-news).
- **Political survival.** Netanyahu campaigned on posters reading "It's the right, or Palestine", showing Arab leaders beside opposition leaders. Breaking a public promise hands him the betrayal story for the next election.
- **Governability.** The 2021 government fell partly on a West Bank vote. A coalition that depends on parties opposed to Israel's settlement policy may simply be unable to pass laws its Jewish partners see as basic security, which is a practical constraint, not only a prejudice.

Netanyahu's own record complicates the taboo. He held talks with Abbas in earlier years, the Guardian notes, before calling the idea treasonous this time. On 28 October, Likud went further and [accused the Arab parties](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/oct/28/israel-election-results-live-updates-exit-polls-netanyahu-likud-eisenkot-latest-news) of having "stolen" the election, without public evidence so far; the elections committee said it would review all complaints as it does after every vote.

## What Arab voters want

This time, many of them came. In Rahat, turnout had reached 72.2% by 6 p.m., against a final 59.5% in 2022, the [Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/israel-election-2026/article-913061) reported, and other Arab cities showed the same pattern. The Israeli site [JFeed](https://www.jfeed.com/news/arab-sector-turnout-80-percent-israel-election) put overall Arab turnout at about 80%, above the national 77.7%; I could not confirm that figure from official data, so treat it as unconfirmed. Netanyahu, on election day, called it "an emergency" and urged right-wing voters out. Reporting from the Bedouin city of Rahat, the [Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/hes-united-arab-israelis-ben-gvirs-harsh-policies-drive-high-turnout-in-arab-towns/) heard voters list three grievances: more demolitions of Bedouin homes under National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir, the failure to curb gun crime, and the reversal of budgets Ra'am won in 2021. One man put it bluntly: "We hate Ben Gvir but are also grateful to him. He's united Arab Israelis." Bedouin women who had never voted before came out too. These are voters' own accounts, not a survey, but they match the turnout numbers.

The crime point is not rhetoric. The [Abraham Initiatives](https://abrahaminitiatives.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2025-Crime-and-Violence-Report.pdf) counted 252 Arab citizens killed in violence and crime in 2025, the deadliest year on record, including a record 23 women; 88% were shot. For many Arab families, the next government is first of all a question of whether their sons and daughters come home alive.

Their leaders are not asking for the same thing. Joint List chair Yousef Jabareen said his party is "ready and willing to pave the way" for Netanyahu's removal, through "real, equal partnership". Abbas is again offering to join. Both may set prices that the Jewish centre-right finds hard to pay.

## Seen from outside

The result also echoes beyond Israel. In Gaza, residents interviewed by AP and quoted in the [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/oct/28/israel-election-results-live-updates-exit-polls-netanyahu-likud-eisenkot-latest-news) hoped for anyone but Netanyahu while doubting much would change; one said Eisenkot's policies were "almost similar". In Egypt, some voiced disappointment that the far right had not been clearly defeated. US President Donald Trump, who endorsed Netanyahu, claimed credit for his late gains.

## The scenarios

Analysts quoted by [JTA](https://www.timesofisrael.com/change-bloc-alliance-with-arabs-unity-government-or-new-election-3-scenarios-for-what-comes-next/) see three paths. First, the opposition governs with Arab parties inside, or as a minority government with their votes from outside, the Rabin model. Second, a "unity" government in which Netanyahu's Likud joins some opposition parties, possibly dropping a far-right or ultra-Orthodox partner. Third, another election, which Plesner suggests may suit Netanyahu, who stays caretaker prime minister until then. A fourth, smaller move is already [under discussion](https://www.timesofisrael.com/as-change-bloc-falls-far-short-of-majority-democrats-golan-urges-coalition-with-arabs/): using the combined 67 votes only to elect a new Knesset speaker, which would be a quiet test of cooperation without a coalition.

## Opinion: a citizen's vote should count the same

I don't think anyone outside Israel should pretend this is easy. After 7 October, fear is real, and a party that will not call Hamas a terror organisation asks a lot of its partners. It is also fair to say that Arab parties have their own red lines, and that some of them are hostile to the state's Jewish character.

But a democracy cannot keep a fifth of its citizens in permanent reserve, counted when they help and erased when they are inconvenient. As Golan pointed out, Netanyahu's own bloc includes ultra-Orthodox parties whose spiritual leaders partly reject Zionism, and their seats are counted without anyone asking for a loyalty test. Rabin showed a middle path thirty years ago: outside support, written conditions, no ministries. If Israel's leaders choose a third election rather than that, they should say plainly that they are choosing to set aside 19 seats of voters, including the families of the 252 people killed in Arab towns last year, who came to the polls asking the state to protect them.
