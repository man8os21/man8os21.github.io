Small and middle-sized states rarely get to choose the world they live in. They choose how to live in it. Greece is a good laboratory for that question. It is a member of the European Union, the eurozone and NATO. It has a large, more populous neighbour, Turkey, which is also a NATO ally and also its main security concern. It sits at the junction of the Balkans, the Black Sea approaches, the Middle East and North Africa. And in the past fifteen years it has gone through a debt crisis that nearly pushed it out of the euro, a migration shock, a naval standoff in 2020, a reconciliation with Ankara from 2023, and a fast-changing international system in which American commitment is less predictable than it used to be.

This piece asks two things. First, what has Greek grand strategy actually been? Second, does *neoclassical realism*, a school of international relations theory, explain it well, or does it leave out too much?

## 1. Definitions

**Grand strategy** is the overall logic by which a state links its means (military, economic, diplomatic, social) to its most important ends, above all survival, territorial integrity and autonomy, over the long term. It is broader than military strategy and longer-lived than any one government's foreign policy. A state may not write it down; often it has to be read from patterns of behaviour.

**Neoclassical realism** starts from the realist assumption that states live in an anarchic system, with no world government to protect them, so the distribution of power around them matters most. But it adds a crucial step. Systemic pressures do not turn into policy automatically. They pass through a filter of domestic "intervening variables". Gideon Rose, who gave the school its name in 1998, put it this way: the scope and ambition of foreign policy are driven first by a country's relative power, but the impact of that power is "indirect and complex", because it must be translated through leaders' perceptions and through the state's domestic structure.[^1] Ripsman, Taliaferro and Lobell later systematised the main filters: leader images and perceptions, strategic culture, state–society relations and domestic institutions.[^2] Put simply:

- **Leader perceptions:** how the people at the top read threats and opportunities, which may be wrong.
- **State capacity:** whether the state can extract resources, such as taxes, conscripts and borrowing, and turn them into power.
- **Domestic politics:** coalitions, public opinion, party competition and the electoral cycle, which can push a government toward or away from what the system "demands".
- **Strategic culture:** inherited ideas about who the enemy is, what counts as humiliation, and what force is for.

The key claim is that a structural reading (where you sit in the balance of power) tells you the *pressures*. The domestic filter tells you the *timing, style and sometimes the direction* of the response.

The typology I will use for Greek actions is standard:

- **External balancing:** aligning with others against a threat.
- **Internal balancing:** building your own military and economic strength.
- **Bandwagoning:** aligning *with* the stronger or threatening power to gain safety or rewards.
- **Hedging:** keeping options open with several powers and avoiding firm commitment against any of them.
- **Institutional binding:** using rules and organisations to restrain others, including a stronger rival or a stronger ally, and to raise the cost of coercion.

## 2. A short history of Greek strategic choices

### 2.1 After 1974: the choice of the West, with conditions

The collapse of the military junta in 1974 came together with the Turkish invasion of Cyprus. That double shock shaped everything after it. Greece withdrew from NATO's integrated military command in protest, believing the alliance and the United States had failed to restrain Turkey, and returned only in 1980. The lesson many Greeks drew, and which lives on in strategic culture, was that NATO membership does not protect Greece against a NATO ally.

The answer of Konstantinos Karamanlis was Europe. Greece joined the European Community in 1981, ahead of Spain and Portugal. Membership was partly about democracy and economics, but it was also a security bet: inside the Community, Greece would have a veto, a voice, and partners whose interest in Greek stability would be economic as well as strategic. Andreas Papandreou arrived in 1981 with anti-NATO and anti-EC rhetoric, but in office he kept Greece in both and used membership as leverage. That gap between rhetoric and conduct is itself a neoclassical realist datum: the system imposed limits, and domestic politics shaped the noise around them.

### 2.2 The 1990s: crises and Europeanisation

The 1990s brought the Imia/Kardak crisis of 1996, which nearly led to armed conflict over two uninhabited islets, and Turkey's 1995 parliamentary resolution that an extension of Greek territorial waters in the Aegean beyond six nautical miles would be a *casus belli*. Greece responded in two ways. It kept spending heavily on defence, which was internal balancing. Under Costas Simitis it also chose "Europeanisation": at the Helsinki summit of 1999 Athens lifted its objection to Turkey becoming an EU candidate, betting that the accession process would bind Turkey to European norms and push disputes toward the International Court of Justice. This is a textbook case of institutional binding. Euro membership in 2001 deepened the anchor.

### 2.3 The 2010s: the crisis as a strategic event

The sovereign debt crisis was not only economic. Between 2010 and 2018 Greece received €288.7 billion in loans over three adjustment programmes.[^3] Its economy shrank by about a quarter, its defence budget was cut, and for some years its policy autonomy was shared with its creditors. In 2015 the SYRIZA government held a referendum rejecting a bailout package and then accepted a harsher one weeks later. The episode showed the limits of state capacity and the force of the system: the costs of leaving the eurozone were judged greater than the costs of staying.

The crisis also widened the menu. China's COSCO took a majority stake in the Piraeus Port Authority in 2016. That was later cited as Greek "hedging", though it was arguably more about need than design.[^4] At the same time, Athens strengthened ties with Israel and Egypt, and the SYRIZA government signed the Prespa Agreement with North Macedonia in 2018, removing a long-standing Balkan dispute at significant domestic political cost.

### 2.4 The Eastern Mediterranean partnerships

From the early 2010s, gas discoveries off Israel, Cyprus and Egypt created a new regional geometry. Greece and Cyprus built two trilateral formats: one with Egypt, launched in Cairo in 2014, and one with Israel. These later became "3+1" with the United States. Both are still active. The tenth Greece–Cyprus–Israel summit in Jerusalem in December 2025 agreed to meet annually and reaffirmed the 3+1 format.[^5] The Egypt–Cyprus–Greece mechanism has committed to a permanent secretariat in Nicosia.[^6]

The legal turning point came after Turkey signed a maritime memorandum with Libya's Tripoli government in November 2019, which drew a line across waters that Greece claims south of Crete. On 6 August 2020, Greece and Egypt signed a partial delimitation of their exclusive economic zones. Turkey denounced it as "null and void".[^7] Weeks later the Turkish survey ship *Oruç Reis* and the two navies faced off in the summer of 2020, and a Greek and a Turkish frigate collided.

### 2.5 The defence pacts: Washington and Paris

Two agreements in late 2021 are central to the present strategy. The first was with France. On 28 September 2021 the two countries signed a strategic partnership whose Article 2 commits them to give each other aid and assistance "by all appropriate means at their disposal, and if necessary by the use of armed force", if they *jointly determine* that an armed aggression is taking place against the territory of either.[^8] It was announced together with the purchase of Belharra frigates and followed earlier Rafale orders. Analysts noted its limits: it covers territory, not disputed maritime zones, and the "joint determination" leaves France room to judge.[^9]

The second, two weeks later, was the renewal of the US–Greece Mutual Defense Cooperation Agreement. It became indefinite unless terminated with two years' notice, and it added US access to sites including Souda Bay on Crete and Camp Giannoulis at Alexandroupolis.[^10] Alexandroupolis then became an important logistics hub for allied forces moving toward Romania and Ukraine after Russia's 2022 invasion.

### 2.6 Rearmament

Greece has kept defence spending among the highest in NATO as a share of GDP, even after the crisis.[^11] It has ordered F-35s from the United States, and in 2025 the government presented a multi-year procurement plan of about €25 billion.[^12] Part of it, the "Achilles Shield" multi-layered air and missile defence, relies heavily on Israeli systems.[^13] Again, this is internal balancing, but it is also tied to external partners through procurement.

### 2.7 Since 2023: "calm waters" with Turkey

After the February 2023 earthquakes in Turkey, when Greece sent rescue teams quickly, the tone changed. In December 2023 Prime Minister Mitsotakis and President Erdoğan signed the Athens Declaration on Friendly Relations and Good-Neighbourliness. It is a political, not a legally binding, text. It commits both sides to dialogue, to confidence-building measures and to avoiding statements and actions that undermine peace, "without any prejudice to each other's legal positions".[^14] The sixth High-Level Cooperation Council took place in Ankara in February 2026.[^15]

The underlying disputes have not gone away. Turkey has protested Greece's maritime spatial plan.[^16] News reports in 2026 describe Greek plans to extend territorial waters in some areas and continued Turkish warnings. I could only confirm these reports through search-result summaries, because the main agency pages block automated reading, so treat the details as unverified.[^17] The "calm waters" policy is a managed truce, not a settlement.

### 2.8 The current era

The present international system has three features that matter for Athens. First, there is war in Europe and pressure on the Black Sea, which raises the value of Greek ports, airfields and logistics to the alliance. Second, there is instability in the Middle East, from Gaza to the Gulf, which makes Greece's partnerships with Israel and Egypt both more valuable and more politically awkward. Third, there is uncertainty about the long-term American commitment to Europe, which pushes the EU toward a "defence union" and makes bilateral insurance, such as the deal with France, more attractive.

## 3. Typology: what kind of strategy is this?

Putting the episodes side by side gives a fairly clear pattern.

| Instrument | Main examples | Aimed at |
|---|---|---|
| External balancing | France pact (2021), US MDCA, trilaterals with Cyprus, Israel and Egypt, EEZ deal with Egypt | Turkey, primarily |
| Internal balancing | Consistently high defence spending, F-35, Belharra, Achilles Shield | Turkey; also regional missile and drone threats |
| Institutional binding | EU membership, Helsinki 1999, insistence on UNCLOS and the ICJ, EU leverage on Turkey's accession and customs union | Turkey; also Greece's own allies |
| Hedging | COSCO at Piraeus (2016), cautious ties with Russia before 2022, the 2023 rapprochement with Ankara | Uncertainty about allies; avoiding entrapment |
| Bandwagoning | Very limited; arguably close alignment with Washington as the leading power | Rewards and protection rather than a threat |

Two observations. First, the main threat in Greek eyes is not a great power but a neighbour within the same alliance. That is unusual, and it explains why Greece balances *inside* NATO, which is why the French clause mattered so much. Second, there is very little real bandwagoning. Greece does not align with Turkey to be safe from Turkey. The 2023 détente is better read as *hedging plus binding*: lowering the temperature, building economic ties, and anchoring the relationship in a declaration, without conceding any legal position.

## 4. Does neoclassical realism fit?

### 4.1 Where it fits well

**The systemic baseline is visible.** Greece faces a larger neighbour with a bigger economy and population, so a structural realist would predict balancing, both internal and external. That is exactly what we see across five decades.

**Domestic filters explain timing and style.** Several turning points are hard to explain without leaders and politics:

- *Leader perceptions:* Simitis's bet on Helsinki reflected a belief that Europe could transform Turkey. Mitsotakis's détente reflects a reading that Ankara, under economic pressure and seeking Western finance and arms, has incentives to keep the Aegean quiet.
- *State capacity:* the crisis years showed how a fiscally broken state loses strategic room, and why internal balancing paused and then returned once borrowing costs fell.
- *Domestic politics:* Papandreou's anti-Western rhetoric versus pro-Western conduct, the political cost of Prespa, and the constant constraint that any perceived concession to Turkey is electorally risky. These shape *how* governments move, often more than *whether* they move.
- *Strategic culture:* the memory of 1974 explains the persistent distrust of "alliance protection" and the search for bilateral guarantees like the French clause.

**It explains Greek behaviour inside the alliance.** A purely structural theory struggles to say why a state would balance against an ally. Neoclassical realism, with its focus on threat perception, can: what matters is who you *perceive* as threatening, not the formal alliance map.

### 4.2 Where it falls short

**Law and institutions as goals, not only tools.** Greece's insistence on UNCLOS, the ICJ and EU norms is not just a realist tactic. It reflects a genuine identity as a status quo state that sees international law as its natural ally. A liberal institutionalist or constructivist would say the EU has changed what Greece *wants*, not only how it pursues fixed interests. Neoclassical realism can absorb this only by calling it "strategic culture", which risks making the theory unfalsifiable.

**Economics and interdependence.** The debt crisis was not a security event in realist terms, yet it reshaped Greek strategy more than any naval standoff. Theories that centre material power can describe the effect, but they have little to say about markets, creditors and rating agencies as actors.

**Identity and religion.** Relations with Serbia, Cyprus, Russia (before 2022) and Orthodox communities in the region carry cultural weight that a power-based theory reads as noise. Constructivists would say it is part of the signal.

**Prediction.** Neoclassical realism is good at explaining the past and weaker at predicting the next move. Would it have predicted the 2023 détente in 2020, at the height of the *Oruç Reis* crisis? Probably not, unless one already knew that an earthquake and two elections would change leaders' perceptions.

**The role of contingency.** The 2023 thaw began with a natural disaster and with human solidarity in response. Theory can explain why the opening was taken. It does not explain why it appeared.

## 5. Scenarios: reading the next decade without a single lens

It is easy to describe Greek strategy through the eyes of Washington, where Greece is a "reliable ally" and a "logistics hub", and the main question is how it fits US plans for the region. That is one lens. Here are several, each with its own assumptions.

**Scenario A: managed détente holds (the official Athens reading).** Turkey, focused on its economy, its role in Ukraine diplomacy and its Middle East ambitions, keeps the Aegean quiet. Greece rearms but talks. Over years, the two sides narrow the agenda to continental shelf and EEZ delimitation and perhaps reach The Hague. *Risks:* a domestic political shock in either country, or a gas or cable incident, reopens everything.

**Scenario B: armed peace (the sceptical Greek reading).** Ankara's priorities are unchanged. It uses the calm to build up its own navy, drones and missile programmes and to wait for a moment when Western attention is elsewhere, for example a crisis around Taiwan, a worse war in Ukraine, or a US–Europe rift. In this reading, the détente buys time for both sides, and the decisive variable is the military balance at the end of the decade. This is why Athens spends what it spends.

**Scenario C: the Turkish reading.** From Ankara, Greece is the actor changing the status quo. It builds an "encirclement" with Cyprus, Egypt, Israel and France, militarises islands that Turkey says should be demilitarised under past treaties, and uses EU membership to turn bilateral disputes into "EU–Turkey" disputes. Taking this view seriously does not mean accepting it. It means recognising that Turkish leaders' perceptions are also an intervening variable, and that Greek internal balancing can be read as a threat that feeds a security dilemma.

**Scenario D: the American partial retreat.** If the US reduces its presence in Europe, then the MDCA, the 3+1 format and US arms sales become less reliable. Greece leans more on France and the EU and on its own capacity. This scenario favours the French bet of 2021, and it raises the stakes of EU defence integration, where Greece has pushed for limits on Turkish participation in EU defence funding.

**Scenario E: the Middle East drags.** The partnerships with Israel and Egypt bring technology, gas and diplomatic weight, but they also tie Greece to conflicts it does not control. A wider regional war, or an outcome in Gaza that turns European public opinion sharply, could raise the domestic cost of the Israel partnership and test how much Greek policy is shaped by public opinion versus elite strategy.

**Scenario F: great-power competition in the Mediterranean.** China's presence at Piraeus, Russian naval activity, Gulf investment and the India–Middle East–Europe Corridor all compete for Greek geography. Greece could become more valuable to everyone, which raises its bargaining power, but also more exposed to pressure.

None of these is certain. A sensible grand strategy has to be *robust* across them, not optimised for one.

## 6. Evaluation and conclusions

Taken as a whole, Greek grand strategy since 1974 has been coherent in its goals and flexible in its tools. The goals are constant: protect sovereignty in the Aegean and the Eastern Mediterranean, deter Turkey without war, stay anchored in the West and keep the support of Europe. The tools have changed with circumstances: binding in the 1990s, survival in the 2010s, active balancing from 2019 to 2022, and since 2023 a mix of strong balancing and careful hedging toward Ankara.

Neoclassical realism explains this pattern better than its simpler cousins. Structural realism gets the direction right but cannot explain balancing inside an alliance or the sudden changes in tone. Domestic variables fill those gaps. But it fits least well where Greek behaviour is driven by law, identity and economics as ends in themselves, and it is weak at forecasting.

The strategy's main weaknesses are threefold. It depends heavily on allies whose guarantees are conditional. It remains expensive for a state that has been through a debt crisis. And it is vulnerable to domestic politics: any Greek government that looks too soft on Turkey pays electorally, which limits room for compromise even when compromise might serve long-term security.

## Opinion: strength in the service of peace, not a substitute for it

*This section is my opinion.*

I think the Mitsotakis–Erdoğan détente is the most valuable thing in Greek strategy right now, and also the most fragile. Rearmament matters: deterrence keeps the door closed to adventurism, and a state that cannot defend itself does not negotiate as an equal. But weapons alone do not make peace. They give time in which peace can be built, and that time is easy to waste.

I also think it is a mistake to read Greek choices only through an American or "Western alliance" frame. Greece's main question is not how to fit into someone else's grand strategy, but how to keep its own security without making itself a permanent frontline in other people's conflicts, whether in Ukraine, the Middle East or a future US–China contest. The French clause, the EU anchor and the calm with Turkey are all, in different ways, ways of keeping that autonomy.

The ethical side also deserves a place in strategy. Truth means not telling citizens that every disagreement with Turkey is about to become a war, or that every agreement is a betrayal. Respect for the neighbour means seeing that Turkish citizens, like Greek ones, want to live without fear. The willingness to forgive and to accept a fresh start, shown in February 2023 when rescuers crossed the border, is not naïve. History shows it is sometimes what opens the way for later diplomacy. The best Greek grand strategy, in my view, is to be strong enough that no one is tempted to attack, and generous enough that no one feels they need to.
