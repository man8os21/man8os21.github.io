Before dawn on Sunday 22 November, seven explosive sea drones hit the Russian corvette *Merkury* at its berth in the naval harbour of Benghazi, in eastern Libya. Satellite images from the firm Vantor, published by the [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cr894gjz0j0yo), showed the ship moored on Saturday and capsized on Sunday, with only the bow above water. The next day Volodymyr Zelensky [claimed the operation](https://abcnews.com/International/ukrainian-naval-drones-sank-russias-merkury-warship-libya/story?id=138369738) as "exemplary": the corvette was sunk, a Ka-27 helicopter destroyed, and a second ship, the cargo vessel *Angara*, which he said carried weapons, was hit off the coast.

Libya is not at war with anyone, and both of its rival governments condemned the strike. Ukraine says it acted in [lawful self-defence](https://kyivindependent.com/strikes-on-russian-ship-in-libya-are-lawful-self-defense-ukraine-says/) under Article 51 of the UN Charter. So who is right? Can a country fighting a war of survival hit its enemy's warship in a third country's port?

## What happened, and what is still unclear

The *Merkury* is a Steregushchiy-class corvette commissioned in 2023. The eastern administration in Benghazi said it had docked for fuel and supplies on its way back to Russia under an existing maritime agreement. Reuters, citing the Institute for the Study of War, reported that it had stayed [several days](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/ukraine-insists-attack-on-russian-ships-in-libya-was-self-defence) while escorting cargo ships that can carry military supplies to Russia.

Several facts are still disputed. Local authorities quoted by [Anadolu](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/eurasia/kremlin-warns-ukraine-of-painful-consequences-following-attack-on-russian-vessels-in-libya-baltic-sea/4134607) spoke of at least ten injured Russian and Libyan crew members; [RBC-Ukraine](https://newsukraine.rbc.ua/news/kremlin-responds-to-ukraine-s-destruction-1795437902.html) reported that Libyan and Russian citizens were among the dead. I have not found a confirmed casualty figure. Ukraine's security service says the drones were launched from a "mother ship". The Benghazi government says there are signs they came from western Libya, which Tripoli [denies](https://www.france24.com/en/europe/20261124-live-tripoli-demands-explanation-from-kyiv-for-striking-russian-ships-in-libyan-waters). It is also not clear whether the *Angara* was inside Libyan territorial waters when it was hit.

## Two Libyas, one sovereignty

Libya has been split since the civil war that followed 2011. The UN-recognised Government of National Unity (GNU) sits in Tripoli. The east, including Benghazi, is held by Khalifa Haftar's Libyan National Army, which Russia has backed for years. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news-analysis/2026/11/24/could-russia-ukraine-war-threaten-libyas-fragile-reunification-process) cites estimates of 1,000 to 1,800 Russian personnel in Libya. Analysts describe eastern Libya as Moscow's logistics bridge to its forces in the Sahel, a bridge that matters more since Assad fell in Syria. Tripoli, for its part, condemned Russia's invasion in 2022 and has received Ukrainian military training since 2023.

So each Libyan camp is close to one side of the war. Yet on this point they agree: Libya must not become a battlefield for someone else's war. The east asked the UN to investigate. Mousa al-Koni, a member of the Presidential Council, called the attack "terrorist" and spoke of taking it to the UN Security Council.

## What the law of naval neutrality says

The core rules are more than a century old. The [1907 Hague Convention XIII](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/hague13.asp) says that "any act of hostility ... committed by belligerent war-ships in the territorial waters of a neutral Power ... is strictly forbidden" (Article 2), and that belligerents may not use neutral ports "as a base of naval operations" (Article 5). The [San Remo Manual](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/san-remo-manual-1994/article-14-22) of 1994, an expert restatement that navies widely use, says the same thing in modern terms: hostile actions in neutral waters are forbidden, including an "attack on ... objects located in ... neutral waters".

Two technical caveats. Libya is not a party to Hague XIII, though Russia and Ukraine are, so the rule binds Libya's waters mainly as customary law and as plain territorial sovereignty. And Article 2 speaks of "war-ships", not unmanned drones. A [Diplomacy and Law analysis](https://www.diplomacyandlaw.com/post/russian-corvette-merkury) of this case argues that the convention's broader Article 1 and the customary ban cover hostile acts whatever the weapon.

On the face of it, then, an attack inside a Libyan harbour breaks the rule. A Russian warship is a lawful military target on the high seas. In a neutral port it is protected, not because it deserves protection, but because the port does.

## Ukraine's three arguments

**First: eastern Libya was not acting as a neutral.** The rules bind the neutral state too. By default a neutral may let a belligerent warship stay no more than 24 hours and take only enough fuel to reach home (Hague XIII, Articles 12 and 19), unless its own laws say otherwise. If Benghazi let Russian warships use it as a regular logistics stop, Kyiv can argue that the port had stopped being neutral ground.

The law foresees this case. San Remo paragraph 22 says that if a neutral fails to end a belligerent's abuse of its waters, the other side must first notify it, give it reasonable time, and may use force only if the abuse is a "serious and immediate threat" and there is no feasible alternative. There is no public sign that Ukraine notified anyone; Italy's foreign minister said Rome was [told nothing](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/24/italy-watches-worryingly-as-ukraine-strikes-russian-boats-in-libya). Ukraine would answer that the corvette was escorting weapons cargo, so it was part of an ongoing war effort, not a ship on holiday. Whether that meets "immediate" is exactly the kind of question the rule leaves to judgment.

**Second: Article 51 has no borders.** Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha argues that self-defence can be exercised wherever the aggressor's forces are. Some scholars go part of the way. Russell Buchan of the University of Reading [argues](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/self-defence-principle-due-diligence-law-neutrality-russia-ukraine-conflict/) that self-defence can justify breaches of neutrality law, but his argument concerns states that help Ukraine, not strikes inside an uninvolved third state. Self-defence is a right against the attacker. Diplomacy and Law concludes that Ukraine's lawful reason to attack the ship does not by itself give it authority to attack inside Libya.

The closest wider debate is about the "unwilling or unable" test, used to justify strikes on armed groups in countries that will not stop them. At a 2021 UN meeting, [Brazil, China, Mexico and Sri Lanka](https://www.justsecurity.org/75487/self-defense-against-non-state-actors-all-over-the-map/) rejected force on another state's soil without its consent, while the US, UK, Turkey and others defended a broad right. That debate concerns non-state groups, but the split is a guide to how the Benghazi strike will be read: much of the world starts from consent.

**Third: Russia created the danger.** Kyiv says Moscow uses other countries' civilian ports for its war and so exposes them to risk. This argument has moral weight. Legally it cuts both ways: if accepted, any port that ever hosted an enemy warship could become a target.

## Has this happened before?

Yes. In March 1915 British cruisers caught the German cruiser *Dresden* in Cumberland Bay, off the Chilean island of Más a Tierra, and [opened fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_M%C3%A1s_a_Tierra); the crew scuttled her. Britain argued that Chile had been unable to enforce its neutrality and that the ship was still armed. Chile protested, and London sent a ["full and ample" apology](https://thegreatwar.pl/en/sinking-of-the-cruiser-dresden), while noting that German warships had used Chilean waters for months. The attacker kept the military gain and paid with an apology.

## How others see it

Outside Kyiv and Moscow, the reaction has been mostly about Libya, not about Russia. The [African Union](https://www.au.int/en/pressreleases/20261124/auc-chairperson-statement-attacks-and-port-benghazi-libya) condemned the attacks and said "no African country should become a battleground for conflicts originating beyond its borders". The [Arab League](http://www.leagueofarabstates.net/en/news/Pages/NewsDetails.aspx?RID=4268) rejected using Arab states' waters as arenas for international conflicts. [Egypt](https://arabic.cnn.com/middle-east/article/2026/11/23/egyptian-statement-following-an-attack-a-russian-warship-benghazi), Haftar's neighbour and backer, called for respect for Libyan sovereignty. Italy, which supports Tripoli, worries that Russian retaliation could [reignite the civil war](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/europe/italy-warns-ukraine-war-risks-spreading-to-mediterranean-after-attack-on-russian-ship-in-libya/4135138) on its southern doorstep, and that the episode could weaken domestic support for aid to Ukraine. I found no statement from Turkey's foreign ministry, although Turkey intervened militarily in 2019 to stop Haftar taking Tripoli.

Moscow has been restrained in words. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called the attacks on Russian ships a "very dangerous trend" and pointed to Russia's daily strikes on Ukraine. At the Security Council Russia holds a veto, and in my judgment a text condemning either side is unlikely to pass.

## What could happen next

Three scenarios seem plausible. In the first, the strike stays a one-off: Libya protests, Ukraine lowers its profile there, and talks between east and west continue; analysts quoted by Al Jazeera doubt one strike will derail the August deal to hold elections within 24 months. In the second, Russia or Haftar retaliates against Ukrainian personnel or GNU targets in western Libya. RUSI's Jalel Harchaoui says both will feel "compelled to retaliate – somehow", and that would put Libya's fragile truce at risk. In the third, Ukraine keeps striking Russian ships in other ports along Moscow's supply routes, and more countries face the choice between hosting Russian warships and staying out of the war.

## Opinion: a just cause does not make every harbour fair game

Ukraine is the victim of aggression, and Russia has little standing to complain about one ship sunk far from home while it bombs Ukrainian cities every night. It is also true that a warship is most vulnerable in port, and that a host which gives one side regular logistics is not neutral in any serious sense. But neutrality rules exist mainly to protect weak countries from wars they did not start. If a just cause allowed strikes in any port that hosts the enemy, states with much weaker causes would borrow the same logic tomorrow. The Benghazi strike gave Ukraine a real military gain and cost it goodwill in Libya, in Rome and across the African Union. A notice to Libya first, as the law asks, would have cost surprise. It would also have kept the moral high ground that is one of Kyiv's strongest weapons.
