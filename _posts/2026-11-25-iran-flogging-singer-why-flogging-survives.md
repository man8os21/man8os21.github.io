On Monday 23 November 2026, at the sentence enforcement office of the prosecutor in Qom, Iranian officials whipped the singer Parastoo Ahmadi 74 times. Four men from her band and production team received the same number of lashes, [according to their lawyer and the rights group Hengaw](https://hengaw.org/en/content/news-sentences-2026-11-23-18283). Their offence was a concert with no audience: in December 2024 Ahmadi, now 29, sang in a long sleeveless dress and without a headscarf in a historic caravanserai, and posted it on YouTube, where it was [viewed millions of times](https://www.france24.com/en/asia-pacific/20261124-un-condemns-iran-flogging-singer-parastoo-ahmadi).

A criminal court in Qom convicted them in June 2026 of "offending public decency" by publishing "obscene and indecent material", and an appeals court upheld the verdict at the end of September, [the BBC reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cq1j6585l5g6o). They were also banned for two years from artistic work and from leaving Iran. One lawyer told the BBC that the defence had asked for a postponement, but "the enforcement branch insisted on carrying it out immediately".

The UN human rights chief Volker Türk called the flogging "abhorrent" and said that flogging is "absolutely prohibited under international law in all circumstances". Iran, as it usually does, rejects such criticism and says Western countries [should look at their own human rights problems](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/23/iranian-singer-lashes-performing-without-hijab). Today, 25 November, is the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, which makes the case a good place to ask two questions: how does flogging work in Iranian law, and why does a punishment most of the world abandoned still survive?

## How flogging works in Iran's penal code

Iran's Islamic Penal Code allows flogging for [at least 148 offences](https://www.euaa.europa.eu/country-guidance-iran-2025/423-corporal-punishments), according to the EU's asylum agency. The code distinguishes two kinds. *Hadd* punishments are fixed and regarded as set by religious law, such as 80 lashes for drinking alcohol. *Ta'zir* punishments are discretionary and set by the state, and the code grades them by severity: up to 10 lashes, 11 to 30, and 31 to 74 (up to 99 for some "indecent" crimes), as the [English translation of the 2013 code](https://iranhrdc.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf_en/Iranian_Codes/Translation_of_the_new_Penal_Code_Book_1and2__507475863.pdf) sets out.

The number 74 is not random. [Article 638 of the code's book on ta'zir punishments](https://dadasan.ir/laws/penal-code-tazir-1375/638) says that anyone who publicly does something that "injures public decency" can be jailed for ten days to two months or receive up to 74 lashes. Its note on women who appear in public without "Islamic hijab", however, provides only prison or a fine, not lashes. So the court did not punish Ahmadi as an unveiled woman in the street; it treated a filmed performance as published indecent content, which opens the door to the whip. The full judgment has not been published, so exactly which articles the court applied is not confirmed.

She is not the first. In January 2024 the activist Roya Heshmati received [74 lashes in Tehran](https://hengaw.org/en/content/article-24544) for posting a photo of herself without a headscarf. The Iranian rights group HRANA counted [96 people sentenced to a total of 5,041 lashes](https://www.en-hrana.org/annual-statistical-report-of-human-rights-conditions-in-iran-2025/) in its 2025 report, including one woman sentenced to 178 lashes over the hijab. These are minimum counts by an opposition-leaning monitor, since Iran does not publish such figures.

## Why make an example now?

The case looks strange at first, because on the street Iran has been retreating. The stricter "Chastity and Hijab" law, passed by parliament, was halted in December 2024 by the Supreme National Security Council, which [told parliament in 2025 not to promulgate it](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202505252480). President Masoud Pezeshkian said enforcing it [might have led to "war" in society](https://www.africanews.com/2025/08/31/irans-president-says-hijab-law-might-have-led-to-war-in-society/). In September 2026 Gholam-Ali Haddad-Adel, a senior conservative and the Supreme Leader's father-in-law, admitted that because of "war conditions" the authorities [cannot "enter this front the way we should"](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202609096713). Reuters notes that the rules are increasingly flouted and enforcement has become "more selective".

Selective is the key word. A government that cannot afford mass confrontations with millions of women can still punish a few prominent ones, and a famous singer is the most visible target there is. That serves two audiences: it tells hardliners, who accuse the state of "retreat", that the law still bites, and it tells every Iranian woman with a microphone what can happen. Public solo singing by women has been restricted since 1979 through [licensing rules rather than a single statute](https://www.theguardian.com/world/iran-blog/2014/aug/29/women-singing-islamic-republic-iran), so Ahmadi broke two taboos at once: her uncovered hair and her voice.

The four men were punished too, but the trigger in every account was the woman's appearance.

Iranian society is not of one mind, and neither are its clerics. In a [2020 online survey by GAMAAN](https://results.gamaan.org/en/question/religious-practice-hijab-mandatory-2020-june), a Netherlands-based group, about 72% of respondents opposed compulsory hijab; its method (anonymous online sampling) is debated, and there is no official poll to compare. Within the clergy, some argue that an Islamic state may enforce the hijab as "commanding right and forbidding wrong", while others cite the Quranic "no compulsion in religion" and argue that faith forced by law loses its meaning, as a [televised debate between two religious scholars](https://3danet.net/en/mehdi-nasiri-and-hossein-sozanchi-debate-the-compulsory-hijab-law-on-zavieh) showed. A survey of 41 senior jurists by the Persian site [Iran Think Tanks](https://iranthinktanks.com/examining-the-opinions-of-taqlid-authorities-on-the-issue-of-compulsory-hijab/) found most accept compulsory hijab and a minority reject it; I could not confirm that count elsewhere. After the flogging, artists inside and outside Iran, as well as Iran's Music House, a professional body, [condemned the sentence](https://www.bbc.com/persian/articles/cm5y178pn78ko).

## Where flogging survives, and where it was cut back

Iran is not alone, and the map does not divide simply into "Islamic" and "Western":

- **Afghanistan:** since the Taliban's leader ordered full application of hudud punishments in 2022, the UN mission recorded corporal punishment against [656 people between January and September 2025](https://www.euaa.europa.eu/coi-report-afghanistan-country-focus/128-capital-and-corporal-punishments), including 131 women and five girls, often for "moral crimes" such as a woman running away from home.
- **Aceh, Indonesia:** a semi-autonomous province allowed to apply Islamic law under the 2005 peace deal. In May 2026 fifteen people were caned in one session; the only woman, convicted of adultery, received [100 strokes while the men got six to ten for gambling](https://thediplomat.com/2026/05/indonesias-aceh-province-continues-caning-as-a-form-of-punishment/). The rest of Indonesia does not cane.
- **Malaysia:** state Islamic courts can order caning; in 2018 two women were [caned in a Terengganu courtroom](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/two-malaysians-caned-in-public-for-attempting-lesbian-sex-idUSKCN1LJ0MG/) for "attempting" sex with each other.
- **Singapore:** a secular state that canes men for serious crimes. Its home affairs minister told parliament in 2023 that caning serves ["deterrence, and proportionate punishment"](https://www.sgparliament.com/debate/1125), without citing any study measuring its deterrent effect.

Two Muslim-majority states moved the other way. In 2020 Saudi Arabia's top court [ended flogging as a discretionary punishment](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/saudi-arabia-to-end-flogging-as-form-of-punishment-document-idUSKCN2262UV/), replacing it with prison or fines; the [Library of Congress notes](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2020-08-03/saudi-arabia-flogging-and-death-abolished-as-tazeer-penalties/) the change concerned ta'zir penalties. The same year Sudan's transitional government [removed flogging for many offences](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2020-07-23/sudan-new-law-amending-penal-code-takes-effect/), but kept it for those treated as hudud, such as drinking alcohol. Both show that a state can cut flogging back without renouncing Islamic law, because most floggings fall under the discretionary category that the state itself controls. Iran's case was ta'zir.

## The arguments for keeping it

Defenders give three reasons. The first is religious: hadd punishments are seen as divinely fixed, and abolishing them looks like abandoning the faith. The second is deterrence and shame: Aceh's officials say public caning makes people "more orderly", though a local human rights scholar says its effect [has never been properly tested](https://thediplomat.com/2026/05/indonesias-aceh-province-continues-caning-as-a-form-of-punishment/). The third is a practical one that even some Western criminologists make: the American sociologist Peter Moskos argued in [*In Defense of Flogging*](https://petermoskos.com/files/moskos/moskos_2014_APJ_flogging.pdf) that years in prison may wreck a life more than a short, painful punishment does. His point was mainly a critique of mass incarceration, and he proposed flogging only as a choice offered to the offender, not a sentence for singing.

None of these reasons fits Ahmadi's case well. No one was harmed, so there was nothing to deter beyond expression itself, and she was not offered a choice.

There is also the legal side. Iran [ratified the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights in 1975](https://tbinternet.ohchr.org/_layouts/15/TreatyBodyExternal/Treaty.aspx?CountryID=81&Lang=en), whose Article 7 bans cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment, and the UN Human Rights Committee has held that whipping violates it, for example in [a 2002 case against Jamaica](https://www.refworld.org/jurisprudence/caselaw/hrc/2002/en/14046). Iran has not joined the Convention against Torture, and it argues that its law follows Islamic principles its people accepted. That disagreement will not be settled by statements from Geneva.

## What could happen next

Three paths seem plausible. In the first, the current pattern continues: lax street enforcement while the war lasts, with exemplary sentences against well-known women. In the second, hardliners use the end of the war to revive the Chastity and Hijab law, risking the confrontation Pezeshkian warned about. In the third, the judiciary quietly narrows discretionary flogging, as Saudi Arabia did, without any public admission. The first looks most likely in the short term; the third would need a political decision at the top that there is no sign of yet.

## Opinion: a whip is not an answer to a song

Prison is not gentle either, and outside condemnation, especially from governments with their own record, can help Tehran's hardliners present every critic as an enemy. But flogging a woman for her voice and her hair is not a dispute about penal theory. It is a state using physical pain to teach women where their place is, and it is violence against women in the most literal sense. The most persuasive pressure will not come from foreign ministries. It comes from Iranian artists, from a president who admits the law would split society, from clerics who say coerced faith is worthless, and from Muslim-majority states that have already put the whip away. Saudi Arabia showed that ending discretionary flogging takes one decision. Iran could make it tomorrow.
