The first summit of the American, Russian and Chinese leaders, held in Shenzhen on 19 November, produced few concrete deals. But one of its four short points was about a court. In the [joint declaration](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2026/11/u-s-russia-china-heads-of-state-declaration-toward-peace/), the three "recognized that the principle of state consent and respect for immunities of State officials are of paramount importance, and concurred that the International Criminal Court and other similar bodies must not abuse their jurisdiction." The declaration named no case and changed no law. Still, it is unusual to see three rivals who agree on very little line up against the same court, one that none of them belongs to.

Why do they all reject it, and does their argument hold up?

## What the court is, and who is outside it

The International Criminal Court (ICC) in The Hague tries individuals, not states, for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression. It was created by a treaty, the Rome Statute, which now has 125 member states. [Ukraine became the 125th](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/icc-welcomes-ukraine-new-state-party) on 1 January 2025. The court acts only when national courts are unable or unwilling to.

The list of non-members is telling. The world's two most populous countries, China and India, are outside, and so are three of the five permanent members of the UN Security Council: the US, Russia and China. Each has its own reasons.

## Washington: "we never agreed to this"

The US signed the treaty in 2000 but never ratified it. The current dispute grew out of the court's Palestine investigation. In November 2024, judges [issued arrest warrants](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/situation-state-palestine-icc-pre-trial-chamber-i-rejects-state-israels-challenges) for Israel's prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu, and former defence minister Yoav Gallant. They had rejected Israel's challenge by relying on the court's jurisdiction over crimes committed on the territory of Palestine, which the court treats as a member state. Whether Palestine can give the court that jurisdiction is itself disputed, and Israel and the US reject it.

[Executive Order 14203](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/02/imposing-sanctions-on-the-international-criminal-court/) of February 2025 answered that neither the US nor Israel is a party or has accepted the court's jurisdiction, and called the warrants baseless. Sanctions followed. By August 2026, [11 current or former judges and officials](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2026/08/un-experts-demand-bold-action-icc-member-states-denounce-new-sanctions) had been designated, including the court's president, Tomoko Akane of Japan. On 9 October the US [sanctioned the institution itself](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/icc-strongly-rejects-us-sanctions-against-institution). The court called this "unprecedented" and gave its own count of thirteen judges and prosecutors targeted.

## Moscow: the warrant for Putin

Russia also signed and never ratified. It [withdrew its signature](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2016/nov/16/russia-withdraws-signature-from-international-criminal-court-statute) in November 2016, shortly after the prosecutor's office described the annexation of Crimea as an occupation. In March 2023 the court [issued a warrant for Vladimir Putin](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/situation-ukraine-icc-judges-issue-arrest-warrants-against-vladimir-vladimirovich-putin-and) and his children's commissioner, Maria Lvova-Belova, for the alleged unlawful deportation of Ukrainian children. For the Kremlin, the "immunities of State officials" clause in Shenzhen is personal: a sitting president should not be arrested when he travels.

That claim is already being tested. When Putin visited Mongolia, a member state, in 2024, he was not arrested. The court [found Mongolia in breach](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/ukraine-situation-icc-pre-trial-chamber-ii-finds-mongolia-failed-cooperate-arrest-and) and rejected its immunity argument.

## Beijing and New Delhi: sovereignty and consent

China has no warrant to fear and still stays out. Its stated concerns, summarised in [academic work](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-10-6129-5_6) and in a [Chinese diplomatic paper](https://icclr.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/IssueofConcern.pdf), are about principle: that the court can reach nationals of states that never joined, that a foreign court gets to judge whether a state is "unwilling" to prosecute, that war crimes in internal conflicts are included, and that an independent prosecutor could bring politically chosen cases. Beijing is also wary of the crime of aggression weakening the UN Security Council, where it holds a veto.

India, a democracy and no ally of Moscow or Washington on this, has [similar objections](https://www.ielrc.org/content/a0505.pdf). It wanted an opt-in system, and it dislikes the Security Council's power to refer and defer cases. It also failed to get terrorism and the use of nuclear weapons added as crimes. This matters because it shows that "the ICC infringes sovereignty" is not only a line used by leaders facing warrants. Many large states hold the view sincerely.

## The legal core: can a court ignore a president's immunity?

Under customary law, sitting heads of state usually enjoy immunity before other countries' courts. Article 27 of the Rome Statute says official position is no defence before the ICC. The hard question is what happens when a member state is asked to arrest the leader of a non-member.

In 2019 the court's Appeals Chamber [ruled in a case about Jordan](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/al-bashir-case-icc-appeals-chamber-confirms-jordans-non-cooperation-reverses-decision) that Jordan should have arrested Sudan's then-president, Omar al-Bashir, and that customary law gives no immunity before an international court. The [African Union disagreed](https://au.int/en/pressreleases/20180910/au-makes-oral-submission-appeals-chamber-international-criminal-court), arguing that another provision, Article 98, obliges states to respect such immunities. The Shenzhen text takes the African Union's side of an argument that serious lawyers still have.

## The Global South's own grievance

Some of the court's harshest critics are members. Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger have filed to leave, with effect in June 2027. [Niger's notice](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/CN/2026/CN.251.2026-Eng.pdf) says the court "raised great hopes" but "has been misused and exploited". In the court's first decade, [every person it charged was African](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-africa-17513065), while powerful states and their allies seemed out of reach. Defenders reply that [several of those cases were referred by African governments themselves](https://theconversation.com/international-criminal-court-is-not-just-for-hunting-africans-18072), but the perception of a double standard stuck. The warrants against Putin and Netanyahu were partly an answer to that charge of selectivity. It is an irony that those are exactly the cases the great powers now object to. [Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/07/burkina-mali-niger/) warns that leaving the court mostly removes a last resort for victims in the Sahel's own conflicts.

The exits are not all one way. Hungary announced it would leave in 2025, then [reversed that decision](https://www.reuters.com/world/parliament-approves-law-keep-hungarys-membership-international-criminal-court-2026-05-27/) in May 2026.

## Does the court actually work?

Critics point to the court's slow pace and its dependence on states to make arrests. Both are real problems. The evidence on deterrence is mixed but not empty. A widely cited [2016 study by Hyeran Jo and Beth Simmons](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/bsimmons/files/josimmons_deteratrocity_io_2016.pdf) found that the court can deter some violence against civilians under certain conditions: most clearly for governments where ratification comes with domestic legal reform and strong local rights groups, and for rebel groups that seek international legitimacy. It is not proof that the court stops wars. It is evidence that "it achieves nothing" is too strong.

The court is also still trying people. Rodrigo Duterte, the former president of the Philippines, goes on trial for crimes against humanity [on 30 November](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/opening-duterte-trial-30-november-2026-practical-information). The Philippines left the court in 2019, but the [Appeals Chamber confirmed](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/icc-appeals-chamber-confirms-jurisdiction-duterte-case) that the court can judge alleged crimes from the years when the country was still a member, 2011 to 2019.

## What happens next

The [Assembly of States Parties](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/twenty-fifth-session-assembly-states-parties-be-held-hague) meets in The Hague from 30 November to 7 December and will [elect six judges](https://asp.icc-cpi.int/elections/judges/2026). The [EU](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/europe/eu-reiterates-firm-support-for-hague-based-court-following-trump-putin-xi-declaration/4131120) has restated its support, and the [UN](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/world/un-defends-international-criminal-court-as-crucial-for-international-justice-after-trump-xi-putin-target-tribunal/4131453) has called the court crucial. There are three plausible paths. Members could close ranks and fund the court despite US sanctions. A slow drift of quiet non-cooperation could take hold, with leaders under warrant simply travelling to friendly members. Or more states could leave, following the Sahel. A temporary US licence that allows some court operations [runs to 7 April 2027](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/937186/download), which makes that date the next pressure point.

## Opinion: the court's flaws are real, and so is the self-interest

The sovereignty argument deserves respect. A court that can reach citizens of states that never signed up is a real departure, and India's and China's objections predate any warrant. The African grievance about selectivity was largely fair for two decades.

But the Shenzhen clause does not come from that tradition. Two of its three authors are reacting to warrants against themselves or a close ally (and Washington also fears cases touching its own soldiers), and the third, Beijing, gets a free statement of principle. "Immunities of State officials" sounds abstract until you recall that the Putin warrant is about children taken from their families. A rule that the powerful must consent before they can be judged is a rule that, in practice, protects the powerful.

The better answer to the court's flaws is to fix them: faster trials, cases taken against the strong as readily as the weak, and member states that actually make arrests. Three non-members telling the court where its limits lie is not that answer.
