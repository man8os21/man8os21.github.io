On 23 November 2026 Greece reopened the Old Acropolis Museum, a small building on the Acropolis rock itself that had been closed for nearly 19 years. Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis used the moment to draw his hardest line yet on the Parthenon Sculptures held in London: "No solution, no agreement can be reached if it recognises foreign ownership," he said, [according to Greek City Times](https://greekcitytimes.gr/greek-news/mitsotakis-rules-out-british-ownership-parthenon-sculptures-deal/). That closes off the idea most often floated in recent years, a long-term loan that would leave the sculptures legally the property of the British Museum.

Three weeks earlier, the British government had said the opposite thing just as clearly. A culture minister told the House of Lords that the government has "no plans to change the law or introduce legislation to permit the Parthenon sculptures to be returned permanently", while welcoming talks on a loan ([The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/politics/elgin-marbles-greece-return-british-museum-b3078585.html)).

So both sides say they want the sculptures to spend time in Athens, and both have made ownership the one thing they will not give. To see why a single legal word blocks everything, you have to go back to 1801.

## How the sculptures got to London

The Parthenon was built between 447 and 432 BC ([YSMA](https://www.ysma.gr/en/monuments/parthenon/)). By 1800 Athens had been part of the Ottoman Empire for more than three centuries, and the temple was already badly damaged, above all in 1687, when a Venetian shell ignited gunpowder the Ottoman garrison had stored inside. Thomas Bruce, the 7th Earl of Elgin, was Britain's ambassador in Constantinople. Between 1801 and the following decade his agents removed a large part of the surviving frieze, metopes and pediment figures and shipped them to Britain. How large is itself disputed: the British Museum says the surviving sculpture is divided "roughly equally" between Athens and London, while Greece's culture ministry says, for example, that [56 of the 97 surviving frieze blocks](https://www.culture.gov.gr/en/parthenonas/SitePages/view.aspx?iID=7) are in London.

In 1816 a House of Commons select committee examined the collection, and Parliament bought it for £35,000, about half of what Elgin had asked, and vested it in the British Museum ([1816 Act](https://statutes.org.uk/site/the-statutes/nineteenth-century/1816-56-george-3-c-99-the-elgin-marbles-act/); [committee report](https://collections.soane.org/b8912)).

The legal argument has turned ever since on one document. Elgin's team obtained an Ottoman authorisation in 1801, but the original has never been found; only an Italian translation survives. Scholars [disagree](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2762&context=clr) on whether it was a formal *firman* and whether it allowed anything more than drawing, measuring, making moulds and digging. In 2024 an official of Turkey's culture ministry, speaking at a UNESCO committee meeting rather than issuing a formal government ruling, said that no firman authorising the removal had been found in the Ottoman archives ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/greece-turkey-britain-parthenon-elgin-marbles-e147bfb7070aba568630c7798b0e7c63)).

The British Museum's trustees answer that Elgin "acted with the full knowledge and permission of the legal authorities of the day in both Athens and London", and that the 1816 committee found his actions "entirely legal" ([Trustees' statement](https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/british-museum-story/contested-objects-collection/parthenon-sculptures/parthenon)). Greece replies that the legal authorities of the day were an occupying empire, and that Greeks had no say.

## Why the British Museum says it cannot give them back

The block is not only political. Section 5 of the [British Museum Act 1963](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1963/24/section/5) lets the trustees dispose of objects only in narrow cases: duplicates, certain printed matter made after 1850, and objects judged unfit to keep or ruined by damage or pests. The Parthenon Sculptures fit none of these, so the trustees could not hand them over even if they wanted to; only Parliament can change that. Parliament has added exceptions before, for objects looted in the Nazi era and for human remains under 1,000 years old, as the minister noted in the Lords. But it has made none for these sculptures.

A newer law, the Charities Act 2022, lets charities return objects on moral grounds. When the government finally switched those powers on in November 2025, it [excluded the national museums](https://www.museumsassociation.org/museums-journal/news/2026/01/dcms-to-review-exclusion-of-national-museums-from-new-restitution-rules/), including the British Museum. After a parliamentary committee criticised that choice, the culture department promised to review the exclusion by February 2027.

What the museum *can* do under current law is lend. And a loan is where the trap lies: a normal loan agreement says who owns the object, and the borrower promises to send it back.

## Why Greece will not sign a loan

For Athens, accepting a loan would mean signing a document that recognises the British Museum's title, something no Greek government has done since Greece first formally asked for the sculptures in 1983. The sculptures are a national symbol, and any Greek government that signed away the claim would pay for it at home. Greek officials, as reported by the Telegraph and summarised by [ProtoThema](https://en.protothema.gr/2026/09/23/greece-and-british-museum-talks-on-parthenon-sculptures-collapse-over-ownership-telegraph-reports/), rejected the model of the Bayeux Tapestry, which France has [lent to the British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/press/press-releases/bayeux-tapestry-displayed-british-museum) until July 2027 in exchange for the Sutton Hoo treasure and Lewis chessmen going to Normandy. The difference, they say, is that nobody disputes France's ownership of the tapestry.

Talks that began in 2021 explored a "hybrid" deal, neither a transfer nor an ordinary loan, in which many sculptures would go to Athens for a long period while Greece sent other antiquities to London. By 2024 they were described as advanced. In September 2026 the Telegraph reported that regular contact had stopped. In October the museum's chair, George Osborne, [proposed](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2026/10/15/british-museum-george-osborne-parthenon-marbles-alternating-display-athens-london) that the sculptures alternate between Athens and London, while insisting "they came here legitimately". According to the Telegraph, Greek officials called that a no-go.

## The case for keeping them in London

The museum's argument deserves to be heard on its own terms. Its trustees say the sculptures in London show ancient Athens "in the context of world history", next to Egypt, Assyria and Persia, seen free of charge by millions of visitors a year, and that two museums telling "complementary but different" stories is a public benefit. About half of the Parthenon's sculpture is lost, and what survives is spread across museums in five countries, including the Louvre and Copenhagen.

There is also the precedent worry. Egypt's best-known archaeologist has led a petition for the [Rosetta Stone](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/egyptologists-call-on-british-museum-return-rosetta-stone-again-2708336), and in 2002 eighteen major museums in Europe, the US and Russia signed a [declaration](http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/entertainment/2558359.stm) defending "universal museums" against restitution claims. If the most famous case goes, the fear runs, the rest will follow.

## The case for reuniting them

Greece's main argument is not about ownership papers but about the object. The sculptures are pieces of one building that still stands. The [Acropolis Museum](https://www.theacropolismuseum.gr/en/exhibit-halls/parthenon-gallery), opened in 2009, displays the frieze in its original order within sight of the temple, with white plaster casts marking the pieces held in London. Other holders have already sent pieces back: Palermo's [Fagan fragment](https://www.theacropolismuseum.gr/en/exhibition-programs/permanent-reunification-fagan-fragment-acropolis-museum) in 2022 and three [Vatican fragments](https://www.theacropolismuseum.gr/en/exhibition-programs/permanent-reunification-parthenon-fragments-vatican-museums-acropolis-museum) in 2023.

The international climate has also moved. In 2021 a UNESCO intergovernmental committee called Greece's request "legitimate and rightful" and urged the UK to negotiate ([summary by a British pro-return group](https://parthenonuk.com/the-icprcp-s-new-decision-recognises-the-intergovernmental-nature-of-the-request-for-the-parthenon-marbles/)). Germany agreed in 2022 to transfer ownership of its 1,130 [Benin Bronzes](https://www.dw.com/en/germany-returns-benin-bronzes-to-nigeria/a-62323704) to Nigeria. Greece also points to stewardship: in 1937–38 an [unauthorised cleaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1937%E2%80%931938_Elgin_Marbles_cleaning_scandal) with copper tools and abrasives stripped surface layers from many of the London pieces. Critics of the slippery-slope argument point out that formal requests to the British Museum have been [few](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2024/01/02/the-parthenon-marbles-and-the-myth-of-the-slippery-slope).

British opinion leans the same way. A YouGov poll of 2,452 adults in October 2026, commissioned by the pro-return Parthenon Project, found 52% for return and 20% for keeping them in London, with 58% backing a return combined with Greek loans to UK museums ([results](https://parthenonproject.co.uk/results-of-new-yougov-survey-commissioned-by-parthenon-project/)).

## What could happen next

**Stalemate.** The most likely outcome for now. The sculptures stay in London, possibly moved within the museum during renovation.

**A loan with a "without prejudice" clause.** Both sides sign a text stating that neither gives up its legal position. This is the hybrid idea, and it fails if Athens reads Mitsotakis's words literally.

**Parliament changes the law.** It has done so for Nazi-era loot. The government says it has no plans, but governments change, and the February 2027 review keeps the debate open.

**Court.** Greece looked at suing in 2015 and [chose not to](https://www.bbc.com/news/uk-32735410), partly because a loss could end its claim for good.

## Opinion: the word "ownership" is doing too much work

Reunification in Athens is the stronger case. The Parthenon is one monument, it still stands, and the museum built to show its sculptures stands [about 300 metres](https://www.theacropolismuseum.gr/en/museum-building) from it. The British Museum's world-history story can be told with casts and with the many objects nobody claims.

But both governments have turned a question of display into a question of national pride, and each now uses the other's red line as a reason not to move. London points to a 1963 law that Parliament has already amended when it wanted to. Athens refuses any text with the word "loan" in it, even though a long, renewable arrangement with an explicit clause protecting its claim could put the sculptures back beside the temple far sooner than waiting for London to concede title. The honest compromise is to let the marble move first and leave the paperwork to argue about later.
