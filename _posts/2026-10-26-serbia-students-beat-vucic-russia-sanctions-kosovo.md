On the night of 25 October 2026, Aleksandar Vučić, who had dominated Serbian politics since 2014, stood at his party headquarters in Belgrade and said: "It is clear that we have lost these elections" ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/25/serbian-leader-aleksandar-vucic-concedes-defeat-in-fiercely-contested-parliamentary-election)). The winner was not a rival party but the Student List, the electoral arm of a protest movement that began after the canopy of the newly renovated railway station in Novi Sad collapsed on 1 November 2024 and killed 16 people.

Much of the coverage abroad treats this as Serbia turning West. The winners themselves say something more complicated. They will not impose sanctions on Russia, and they do not accept Kosovo's independence. Here is why, and what it means.

## What happened on Sunday

With 8,077 polling stations processed, Serbia's election commission (RIK) gave the Student List 52.54% and Vučić's United Serbia list 39.29%, on a turnout of 68.78%, the highest in more than 30 years ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/26/serbia-after-vucic-who-will-be-pm-and-when-will-a-government-be-formed), [Beta](https://betabriefing.com/news/politics/36722-student-ticket-wins-vucic-concedes-defeat)). These are preliminary figures; a later BBC count with 97% of stations put the students at 52.7% ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cw5yn3xlprpmo)). Projections give the students about 138 to 141 of 250 seats, so they can govern alone. The Socialist Party of Ivica Dačić, part of every parliament since multiparty politics returned in 1990, fell below the threshold for the first time ([Beta](https://betabriefing.com/news/politics/36722-student-ticket-wins-vucic-concedes-defeat)).

The movement has no single leader by design. Ilija Srdanović, a cardiologist and medical professor from Novi Sad, led the campaign and is seen as the likeliest prime minister, but no one has been named. Parliament must convene within 30 days of the final results, and a government must follow within 90 days ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/26/serbia-after-vucic-who-will-be-pm-and-when-will-a-government-be-formed)).

Its programme is almost entirely domestic: an independent police unit answering to prosecutors, a ban on party loyalists running state companies, a law to seize unexplained wealth from officials, public scrutiny of big infrastructure contracts, and a push against retail monopolies to bring food prices down ([Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/europe/serbia-elections-student-list-eu-russia-b3073569.html)). Vučić and his party have always denied the accusations of corruption and repression.

## The foreign policy that did not change

In September the movement wrote that "the Republic of Serbia will retain its principled stance on not introducing restrictive measures toward the Russian Federation", pointing to Serbia's own history with sanctions ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/serbias-student-victory-raises-hopes-of-an-eu-reset)). A memorandum in May rejected Kosovo's independence. Candidates called Kosovo a "temporarily occupied" region to be reintegrated by peaceful means, and the list rejects earlier Belgrade–Pristina agreements on energy, licence plates and the use of the dinar ([Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/europe/serbia-elections-student-list-eu-russia-b3073569.html)). Leading candidates have also said Serbia would end arms exports to war zones, including sales to Western intermediaries.

This is not a disguise. It reflects four concrete pressures.

**Memory.** On 30 May 1992 the UN Security Council imposed a near-total trade embargo on what was then Yugoslavia, meaning Serbia and Montenegro, through [Resolution 757](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/142881?ln=en). Three months later industrial production had fallen by 40%, and monthly inflation in 1993 reached 313 million percent ([Bajić-Hajduković](https://contemporarysee.org/allfiles/documents/13-remembering-the-embargo-cake-the-legacy-of-hyperinflation-and-the-un-sanctions-in-serbia.pdf)). Economists add an important correction: the sanctions pushed prices up by tens of percent, but the hyperinflation itself came mainly from a government printing money to cover its deficits ([Arsić, 2022](https://doi.org/10.2298/pan2202205a)). Still, as that anthropological study of the "embargo cake" shows, the period lives on in family memory as empty shops and improvised recipes, and "sanctions" remains a loaded word in Serbian politics.

**Energy.** Gazprom is Serbia's largest gas supplier, delivering around 2.2 billion cubic metres a year below market price. The country's only oil refinery belongs to NIS, which is under US sanctions because of its Russian owners; Serbia needs US waivers to keep crude flowing until the Russian stake is sold to Hungary's MOL ([Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/europe/serbia-elections-student-list-eu-russia-b3073569.html)). The students call this "an acute economic dependency", not a geopolitical alliance. Joining EU sanctions now could put the winter gas supply at risk before any alternative exists.

**Kosovo.** Kosovo declared independence in 2008, but it cannot join the UN without a Security Council recommendation, which Russia and China can veto ([OSF Western Balkans](https://osfwb.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Kosovos-path-toward-EU-and-UN-membership.pdf)). The legal framework from 1999, [Resolution 1244](https://press.un.org/en/1999/19990610.sc6686.html), did not settle Kosovo's status. Any Serbian government that keeps the claim needs Moscow's and Beijing's vetoes. As Dimitar Bechev of Carnegie Europe put it, "there are strong nationalist voices in Studentska lista who would want to keep Russia onside because of Kosovo" ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/serbias-student-victory-raises-hopes-of-an-eu-reset)).

**Voters.** Serbia is the least EU-enthusiastic candidate country. The European Commission's 2025 survey found 33% support for accession, against 91% in Albania ([European Commission](https://enlargement.ec.europa.eu/news/special-eurobarometer-and-perception-surveys-2025-09-02_en)). Other polls give 39% ([IRI](https://www.iri.org/news/iri-survey-shows-strong-support-for-european-union-membership-in-four-of-five-western-balkans-countries/)) or a near tie of 46% for and 44% against ([CeSID](https://www.cesid.rs/istrazivanja/podrska-evropskim-integracijama-u-srbiji-polarizovala-javnost/)). One poll found 81% against NATO membership ([N1/Lidington](https://n1info.rs/english/news/most-serbians-oppose-eu-membership-but-see-eu-as-key-economic-partner/)). An online survey by the COMPASS project found 64% against sanctions on Russia; it was not a probability sample, so treat that figure as indicative, not confirmed ([COMPASS](https://serbiacompass.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/CompassAnketa02_250805.pdf)). A movement that won by uniting nationalists, liberals and the apolitical cannot open with the issue most likely to split it.

## Where the money actually is

The paradox is that Serbia's economy already lives in Europe. In 2024, 61% of its exports went to the EU, while Russia accounted for only about 3% of its trade, a twenty-year low ([EKN](https://www.ekn.se/en/countries/countries--continents/compare-countries/serbia/)). China is the biggest single investor, with about 30% of foreign investment inflows in 2022–2024, mostly in mining, metals and car parts, and about 91% of Serbia's exports to China are copper ores and processed copper ([EKN](https://www.ekn.se/en/countries/countries--continents/compare-countries/serbia/), [BMEE 2026](https://doi.org/10.3846/bmee.2026.25457)). So Russia matters for gas and diplomacy, China for factories and mines, and the EU for almost everything else.

## How the others see it

**Moscow** hopes Serbia "will continue not to join Western sanctions", in the words of Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov, and said it still values Vučić. **Beijing** said it is ready to deepen ties with the new government ([DPA](https://www.yahoo.com/news/world/articles/moscow-beijing-maintain-ties-serbia-123332355.html)). **Brussels** was warmer than the students' own words: Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos said "the door to the EU is open". But eight member states blocked the opening of the next group of accession chapters, known as Cluster 3, in July, citing democratic backsliding and foreign-policy alignment ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/serbias-student-victory-raises-hopes-of-an-eu-reset)). Serbia matched only 67% of EU foreign-policy declarations in 2025, by one think tank's count ([ISAC Fund](https://www.isac-fund.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Annual-analysis-of-FP-alignment-2025-ENG.pdf)).

**Hungary's** new prime minister, Péter Magyar, who ousted Viktor Orbán in April, was the first foreign leader to call. **Montenegro's** prime minister hoped for "a new chapter … without unnecessary escalation" ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/serbias-student-victory-raises-hopes-of-an-eu-reset)). In **Kosovo**, Prime Minister Albin Kurti focused on the Albanian minority list in southern Serbia winning seats ([Gazeta Express](https://www.gazetaexpress.com/en/Kurti-reacts-to-Albanian-coalition-running-for-third-term--Vucic-admits-defeat--Dacic-faces-heavy-defeat/)). The students' rejection of past agreements gives Pristina little reason to expect quick progress.

**Ukraine** has a quieter stake. The Financial Times reported in 2024 that about €800 million of Serbian ammunition had reached Ukraine through intermediaries since 2022; Belgrade said it sold legally to third countries, and Russia's foreign intelligence service later accused Serbia of supplying Kyiv ([Ukrinform](https://www.ukrinform.net/rubric-ato/3877795-ukraine-received-eur-800-million-worth-of-ammunition-from-serbia-through-intermediaries-ft.html), [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/russias-spy-agency-says-serbia-sold-ammunition-ukraine-via-bulgaria-czech-2025-06-23/)). If the new government ends exports to war zones as promised, the "pro-Western" students could end up helping Kyiv less than the "pro-Russian" Vučić did.

## Three scenarios

1. **Reform at home, caution abroad.** The government spends its first year on police, courts and procurement, keeps neutrality, and lets the EU judge it on rule of law. This matches what candidates such as former central bank governor Dejan Šoškić have said: close the negotiating chapters first, ask the public about membership later ([Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/europe/serbia-elections-student-list-eu-russia-b3073569.html)).
2. **Split.** The coalition divides between its pro-European and nationalist wings over Kosovo, sanctions or the arms ban, and the majority frays.
3. **Gradual alignment.** Once the NIS sale and new gas routes reduce dependence on Russia, Belgrade quietly matches more EU positions without a dramatic sanctions vote.

None of these is certain. Euronews also recalls that the last reforming prime minister who took on organised crime and the security services, Zoran Đinđić, was assassinated in 2003 ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/serbias-student-victory-raises-hopes-of-an-eu-reset)). The risk is not only political.

## Opinion: judge them by the courts, not the sanctions list

My view is that outsiders asking "is Serbia now with the West?" are asking the wrong first question. Sixteen people died in Novi Sad because a public building was renovated badly and no one answered for it. That is what these voters rose up over, and the test of the new government is whether the next death caused by negligence ends in a trial.

On Russia, I think the students' caution is defensible for now. A government that cuts its own gas in its first winter would hand power back to the people it just beat. But it has a cost they should state honestly: as long as Serbia stays outside EU sanctions and rejects every deal with Pristina, the EU path stays blocked, and the 61% of exports that go to Europe give Brussels more leverage than Moscow's gas does. Neutrality is a choice, not a free lunch.

The strongest sign of change on Sunday was not a slogan. It was a ruler who counted the votes, said he had lost, and promised not to obstruct the new parliament. Serbia has not seen that often. Protecting it matters more than any foreign-policy label.
