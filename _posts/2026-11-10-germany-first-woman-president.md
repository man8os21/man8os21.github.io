On 9 November 2026, Germany's Christian Democrats formally nominated Ilse Aigner, the 61-year-old speaker of the Bavarian state parliament, as their candidate to succeed President Frank-Walter Steinmeier, who cannot run again after two terms. Her own party, the Bavarian CSU, has backed her for months, and the Social Democrats, the third party in the federal coalition, endorsed her the same morning, [according to dpa](https://www.tagesspiegel.de/politik/dpa-wahl-am-30-januar-merz-schlagt-aigner-offiziell-als-bundesprasidentin-vor-16259352.html). If the Federal Convention elects her on 30 January 2027, she will be the first woman to hold the office since the Federal Republic was founded in 1949.

That raises two questions worth taking seriously. Why did a country that was led by a woman chancellor for 16 years never put a woman in its largely ceremonial top job? And does it matter who holds a post with so little formal power?

## How Germany picks its president

Germans do not vote for their president directly. Under [Article 54 of the Basic Law](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/gg/art_54.html), the president is chosen, without debate, by a Federal Convention that meets only for this purpose. It is made up of every member of the Bundestag plus an equal number of delegates chosen by the 16 state parliaments in proportion to their party strength. With 630 Bundestag members, that makes 1,260 electors, [as the Frankfurter Rundschau notes](https://www.fr.de/politik/ilse-aigner-reichinnek-attackiert-merz-suche-nach-bundespraesidentin-zr-94579110.html). A candidate needs an absolute majority in the first or second round; in a third round, the most votes wins. Any candidate must be a German citizen of at least 40, and the term is five years, renewable once.

The vote is secret, so party discipline is not guaranteed. But the CDU, CSU and SPD together hold a majority of roughly 50 votes in the convention, according to dpa, and the Greens have signalled sympathy. A first-round win is widely expected. The new president takes office in mid-March, [DW reports](https://www.dw.com/en/who-is-ilse-aigner-leading-candidate-for-germanys-next-president/a-79898848).

## What the job actually is

The German president is not a figurehead with nothing to do, but the role is limited. The [president's office lists](https://www.bundespraesident.de/DE/amt-und-aufgaben/aufgaben-in-deutschland/amtliche-funktionen/amtliche-funktionen.html) the formal tasks: proposing a chancellor to the Bundestag, appointing ministers on the chancellor's proposal, appointing federal judges and officers, pardoning in federal cases, and signing laws.

That last power has teeth. Before signing, the president checks whether a law is constitutional, and has refused to sign eight times since 1949, most recently twice in 2006 under Horst Köhler. The president can also dissolve parliament in narrowly defined situations: Steinmeier did so in December 2024 after Chancellor Olaf Scholz lost a confidence vote, setting the [snap election of February 2025](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/german-president-dissolves-parliament-pave-way-feb-23-snap-elections-2024-12-27/). Beyond those moments, the power is mostly the power of words: speeches, visits, and the moral authority of being above day-to-day politics.

## Nine women before her, none with a chance

Aigner is not the first woman to run. She is the first to run with a majority behind her. [A survey of earlier candidates by AFP](https://www.tagesspiegel.de/politik/afp-neun-frauen-ohne-sieg-das-sind-die-fruheren-bundesprasidentschafts-kandidatinnen-16254117.html) counts nine: Annemarie Renger for the SPD in 1979, the writer Luise Rinser for the Greens in 1984, Hildegard Hamm-Brücher for the liberal FDP in 1994, Dagmar Schipanski for the CDU and Uta Ranke-Heinemann for the PDS in 1999, Gesine Schwan for the SPD in 2004 and 2009, Luc Jochimsen in 2010, the Nazi-hunter Beate Klarsfeld in 2012 and the physicist Stefanie Gebauer for the Free Voters in 2022. What they had in common, AFP writes, is that each was put forward by parties with no prospect of a majority.

The magazine Cicero made the point bluntly in a piece titled ["the more hopeless, the more female"](https://www.cicero.de/innenpolitik/je-aussichtloser-umso-weiblicher/48493). It argued that from the late 1970s women became fashionable as candidates, but mostly as "count candidates", nominated to lose. Its sharpest example: the CDU fielded Schipanski in 1999 when it knew it would lose, but when it had a safe majority five years later, she was not on the shortlist and the nominee was a man. That pattern is not only German. The political scientist Farida Jalalzai, studying women executives worldwide, found that [women are more likely to reach top office when its powers are few and constrained](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/politics-and-gender/article/women-rule-shattering-the-executive-glass-ceiling/AEA3583A57790A5F2C31C26BFFD8CAE4), and that in Europe many women heads of state have held [weak, largely symbolic presidencies](https://doi.org/10.1177/0192512114542112). Germany is a partial exception: here the powerful job, the chancellorship, went to a woman first, with Angela Merkel from 2005 to 2021, while the symbolic one stayed male. The likely reason is the gatekeeper. A chancellor is chosen through a national election campaign; the president is chosen by a small group of party leaders bargaining over who can carry a majority in the convention.

## The criticism

Not everyone is celebrating. The Left Party's leader, Ines Schwerdtner, said the governing parties were once again "settling the highest office among themselves", [Deutschlandfunk reported](https://www.deutschlandfunk.de/kritik-von-afd-und-linken-an-entscheidung-fuer-aigner-102.html), and the Left's parliamentary leader Heidi Reichinnek called the presidency a post "shuffled back and forth" between the Union and the SPD. The far-right AfD, now the largest opposition group in the Bundestag, said Aigner had shown as Bavarian speaker that she could not shed the role of party politician; it plans to field its own candidate. Aigner in turn declined to present herself to the AfD group, citing a remark by one of its MPs who called her nomination "a testament to poverty", [Deutschlandfunk said](https://www.deutschlandfunk.de/aigner-stellt-sich-im-bundestag-vor-102.html).

Even supporters criticised the process. Green leaders called Chancellor Friedrich Merz's search "unprofessional", and coverage has framed the choice as a deal between Merz and the CSU leader Markus Söder, who first floated Aigner's name in April.

There is a fair defence too. Merz's constraint is arithmetic: without the SPD, his party cannot carry the convention, so any nominee had to be acceptable to a centre-left partner, and a consensus candidate from the centre is what the system rewards. Aigner herself did not frame her candidacy mainly around gender but around social mobility: a trained radio and TV technician who became a federal minister, she said her life stands for the country's "promise of rising", [according to the CDU](https://www.cdu.de/aktuelles/cdu-deutschlands/ilse-aigner-kandidatin-fuer-das-amt-der-bundespraesidentin-und-cdu-bundeskanzler-friedrich-merz/). The Bundestag president, Julia Klöckner, called it a sign of "social normality" that it no longer needs to be remarkable, [Tagesschau reported](https://www.tagesschau.de/inland/innenpolitik/aigner-merz-bundespraesidentin-100.html). Many conservatives would say the right question is whether she is good at the job, not what sex she is.

## Does a woman in a ceremonial post change anything?

Here the evidence is more interesting than the slogans. The best-known study comes from India, where a 1993 law reserved village council leadership for women in randomly selected villages. Using surveys of 8,453 adolescents and their parents, [Beaman, Duflo, Pande and Topalova found](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1212382) that after two terms of a woman leader, the gender gap in girls' aspirations shrank by 32%, the gap in schooling disappeared, and girls spent less time on chores. Because women's job prospects did not change, the authors attribute the effect mainly to role models.

At the national level the evidence is weaker but points the same way. Comparing many democracies, [Wolbrecht and Campbell (2007)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2007.00289.x) found that where more women sit in parliament, adolescent girls are more likely to discuss politics and to say they will take part as adults. But that is about many visible women legislators, not one head of state. A village head who decides where the well goes is seen every day; a president in Berlin is distant, and I could find no causal study of what a woman in a ceremonial national post changes. Globally, women remain rare at the top: [UN Women counts](https://www.unwomen.org/en/what-we-do/leadership-and-political-participation/facts-and-figures) only 20 countries with a woman head of state as of September 2026, and estimates that parity in the highest posts is 130 years away at current rates. Other indirectly elected presidencies show the range. Greece's parliament chose its first woman president, Katerina Sakellaropoulou, in 2020 with [261 votes out of 300](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/jan/22/greek-parliament-elects-countrys-first-female-president). India's electoral college of MPs and state legislators has now chosen two women: Pratibha Patil in 2007 and, in 2022, Droupadi Murmu, [the first president from a tribal community](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/india-elects-first-president-tribal-community-2022-07-21/), whose election was read in India as much through caste and tribe as through gender. A first is never only about one kind of exclusion.

## Opinion: the first should have come sooner, and should not be the last word

I think Aigner's likely election matters, and that the critics also have a point. It matters because for 77 years the people who decided this office kept choosing men whenever they could actually win, and put women on the ballot only to lose. That pattern ends now, and a girl in Germany will grow up knowing the state's highest office is hers to aim for. The Indian evidence suggests that kind of signal is not empty.

But a symbolic first is cheap if nothing follows, and the conservatives are right that sex is not a qualification. The test is what she does with the one real power the office has: words. Germany is not among the five EU countries that, [according to the European Women's Lobby](https://womenlobby.org/press-release-the-eu-ratified-the-istanbul-convention-but-femicide-remains-silent/), make femicide a specific crime. A president who used her platform to make violence against women and children visible, whatever her party, would give the first real content. And the Left has a point that a choice made among coalition leaders does little to rebuild trust in politics. The next time, it should not take a coalition deal for a woman to win.
