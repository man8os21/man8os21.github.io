On Monday 19 October, drones struck Amiet market, a busy trading hub 10 to 15 kilometres north of Abyei town. Fuel stores caught fire. Abyei's caretaker information minister, Yohana Akol Ngor, told [Radio Tamazuj](https://www.radiotamazuj.org/en/news/article/death-toll-from-drone-strikes-on-abyei-market-rises-to-153) that 153 bodies had been buried in two mass graves and that people were still missing. He rejected a higher figure of 220. The toll comes from the local administration and has not been independently confirmed; the [UN mission](https://reliefweb.int/report/sudan/unisfa-statement-drone-strikes-amiet-market-abyei) spoke of "dozens" killed while it was still counting. The same weekend, according to the [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cr3el70jz1nqo), the monitoring group Sudan War Observatory reported army drone strikes in neighbouring West Kordofan that killed about 150 more, including mourners on their way to a funeral.

Abyei is about 10,500 square kilometres, according to [EBSCO's reference entry](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/abyei), and both Sudan and South Sudan claim it. To understand why a market there became a target, you have to start in 1905.

## A colonial pen stroke

In 1905 the Anglo-Egyptian administration moved the nine chiefdoms of the Ngok Dinka from the southern province of Bahr el-Ghazal into northern Kordofan. As the [Max Planck Encyclopedia](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e2090) explains, that left a people ethnically tied to the South on the northern side of the line that later became the border.

The land was not theirs alone. The Misseriya, Arabic-speaking cattle herders, move south through Abyei every dry season for water and grass; for them, access is not a political preference but a matter of whether their herds survive. For decades the two communities shared the land with friction but also cooperation. The Ngok paramount chief Deng Majok and the Misseriya leader Babo Nimir are remembered for settling grazing disputes together, a story told by Deng Majok's son, the diplomat Francis Mading Deng, in [a biography of his father](https://www.umojabooks.com/products/the-man-called-deng-majok).

Sudan's civil wars broke that balance. Many Ngok joined the southern rebels, while Khartoum recruited and armed Misseriya militias, as the [Small Arms Survey](https://www.smallarmssurvey.org/sites/default/files/resources/HSBA-WP-26-Conflict-Dynamics-in-Abyei.pdf) documents. Abyei became a front line.

## The peace deal that postponed the question

The 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement gave the South a referendum on independence. Abyei got its own [protocol](https://www.peaceagreements.org/view/583/), defining it as "the area of the nine Ngok Dinka chiefdoms transferred to Kordofan in 1905", with a separate vote on joining the South or staying with the North.

That definition looked neat and proved explosive, because nobody had mapped the 1905 boundary reliably. An expert commission reported in 2005 that Abyei stretched far north and included oil fields; Khartoum rejected it. The Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague ruled on [22 July 2009](https://pcacases.com/web/sendAttach/19012) that the experts had partly exceeded their mandate and drew smaller limits. According to [ASIL](https://asil.org/insights/volume-13-issue-15/), these left out the large Heglig field but kept at least one working field inside, and both parties said they were satisfied.

Oil raises the stakes. When the South seceded, Sudan lost about 75% of its oil reserves, says the [US Energy Information Administration](https://www.eia.gov/international/content/analysis/countries_long/Sudan_and_South_Sudan/), and much of the rest lies near the shared border.

## Who gets to vote?

The Abyei vote was due in January 2011. It never happened, because the sides could not agree who counts as a resident. In 2012 an African Union panel led by Thabo Mbeki proposed limiting the vote to the Ngok and others with a permanent home in Abyei, while guaranteeing Misseriya grazing, water and movement. Juba accepted. Khartoum [rejected it](https://sudantribune.com/article/43307), arguing it would shut out the Misseriya, long-standing allies of Khartoum. Their fear is understandable: a vote that hands the land to South Sudan could turn a seasonal route into an international border.

In May 2011, weeks before independence, Sudan's army took Abyei by force, and about 110,000 Ngok fled south, the [Small Arms Survey](https://www.smallarmssurvey.org/sites/default/files/Crisis-in-Abyei-March-2013.pdf) estimated. The Security Council then created the [UN Interim Security Force for Abyei](https://press.un.org/en/2011/sc10298.doc.htm) (UNISFA). In 2013 the Ngok held their own vote; [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/thousands-in-contested-abyei-vote-to-join-south-sudan-risking-tensions-idUSBRE99U0VT/) reported 99.9% for South Sudan, but the Misseriya boycotted it and no government recognised it.

Not all violence runs north to south. Since 2022, clashes between the Ngok and the Twic Dinka from South Sudan's side have killed civilians in southern Abyei, [ACLED](https://acleddata.com/update/sudan-situation-update-march-2023-deadly-violence-disputed-abyei-area) records, and the UN has reported South Sudanese uniformed personnel there despite Abyei's [weapons-free status](https://peacekeeping.un.org/en/security-council-briefing-special-representative-of-secretary-general-martha-ama-akyaa-pobee-4).

## Sudan's war arrives

Since April 2023, Sudan's army (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have been at war. A [UN strategic review](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n25/208/64/pdf/n2520864.pdf) found that the population around Amiet had grown from about 10,000 to roughly 50,000, driven by refugees and by war demand for fuel and goods, with RSF personnel present and crime rising from 1,712 incidents in 2023 to 2,373 in 2024. [Sudans Post](https://www.sudanspost.com/who-really-controls-abyei/) published photos it says show RSF fighters loading fuel there in November 2025.

That is the army's case. A senior Sudanese officer told AP, as [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/21/drone-attack-in-disputed-sudan-south-sudan-border-region-kills-at-least-153) reported, that the strike hit fuel tankers bound for the RSF and that the dead were fighters and smugglers. Diplomats told Reuters the depot had supplied RSF fighters before. South Sudan blamed the army and summoned Sudan's ambassador. The UN Secretary-General said women and children were among the dead, without naming the attacker. The RSF did not comment.

## The peacekeepers' deadline

UNISFA has more than 2,800 uniformed personnel, the BBC reports. At Sudan's request its all-Ethiopian force was replaced in 2022 by contingents from Ghana, Pakistan and others, [Security Council Report](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/monthly-forecast/2022-11/sudan-south-sudan-16.php) records.

Its mandate ends on 15 November. The last renewal, [resolution 2802](https://press.un.org/en/2025/sc16223.doc.htm), passed 12 to 0, with China, Russia and Pakistan abstaining. Washington, which drafted it, tied future renewals to benchmarks such as removing unauthorised forces and creating a joint police service, saying they would track progress and hold both host governments accountable. Pakistan, a major troop contributor, replied that the mission's fate was being made to depend "on external factors beyond the mission's control". Guyana, speaking also for Algeria, Sierra Leone and Somalia, called the benchmarks "unrealistic". Denmark and others regretted that references to gender and sexual violence were cut.

Does a mission like this help? A [2013 study](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12036) of African civil wars found that larger deployments of UN troops and police were linked to fewer civilian killings. UNISFA has not settled Abyei's status, and Monday's strike shows what it cannot stop. But status was never its job.

## Three ways it could go

The Council could renew with softer conditions, the likeliest path, which keeps a buffer but changes nothing. It could enforce the benchmarks and shrink the force, risking what Pakistan called "a rapid escalation of intercommunal violence". Or, eventually, status talks could resume, but only once Khartoum has a government able to negotiate and deliver, which is not in sight.

## Opinion: a market is not a fuel tank

I accept that the RSF uses Abyei as a supply line and that an army at war will try to cut it. But [humanitarian law](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule14) forbids attacks whose expected civilian harm is excessive compared with the military gain, and a late-morning strike on fuel inside a market of tens of thousands looks hard to justify under that test. The deeper failure is older: a colonial line and a peace deal that left one question, who belongs here, to be fought over instead of decided. Neither community chose this. The women who trade at Amiet and the children beside them deserve a mission that stays and a voting rule both peoples can live with, one that protects Misseriya grazing whatever the outcome.
