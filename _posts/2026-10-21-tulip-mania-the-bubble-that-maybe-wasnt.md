Whenever prices of anything run wild, someone mentions tulips. Dutch traders in the 1630s, the story goes, paid the price of a house for a single flower bulb, chimney sweeps joined in, and when the market crashed in February 1637 a whole nation was ruined. It opens almost every book on bubbles.

The trouble is that the familiar story was written two centuries later, and the scholars who went back to the records found something much smaller and stranger. Here is what we know, where the legend came from, and why economists still argue about whether it was a bubble at all.

## The legend, in its own words

The popular version comes from the Scottish journalist Charles Mackay, whose 1841 book [*Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24518) is free to read today. Mackay wrote that in 1634 "the rage among the Dutch to possess them was so great that the ordinary industry of the country was neglected, and the population, even to its lowest dregs, embarked in the tulip trade."

He lists a single Viceroy bulb traded for a basket of goods worth 2,500 florins: wheat, rye, four fat oxen, eight fat swine, twelve sheep, wine, beer, butter, a thousand pounds of cheese, a bed, a suit of clothes and a silver cup. He says someone offered twelve acres of building land for a Semper Augustus bulb. And he describes the end: "Substantial merchants were reduced almost to beggary, and many a representative of a noble line saw the fortunes of his house ruined beyond redemption."

It is vivid writing, and mostly secondhand. According to the [Wikipedia summary of the scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_mania), Mackay leaned largely on a 1797 work by Johann Beckmann, and Beckmann's sources drew heavily on a satirical pamphlet, the *Dialogues between Waermondt and Gaergoedt*, written just after the crash to mock the speculators.

## What was really being traded

The basic facts are not in dispute. Tulips came to Europe from the Ottoman Empire, and the botanist Carolus Clusius planted them in Leiden in the 1590s. The most prized flowers were the "broken" tulips with flame-like stripes on their petals. As [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/there-never-was-real-tulip-fever-180964915/) explains, that pattern was later found to come from a mosaic virus, which also made the bulbs sickly and slower to multiply. So the most beautiful flowers were also the hardest to breed, which is a recipe for scarcity.

Tulips bloom for only about a week in spring. Bulbs can only be dug up and moved in summer. So for most of the year, traders were not buying bulbs at all. They were signing contracts to buy bulbs that were still in the ground, to be delivered after the season. In modern terms these were forward contracts. The Dutch called it *windhandel*, "trade in wind," because nothing changed hands. Deals were struck in taverns, no deposit was required from either side, and every contract was a private promise between two people.

That detail matters. Contract prices peaked in the winter of 1636 to 1637. A contemporary satire says the collapse began on 3 February 1637 at a Haarlem auction where no buyer would bite. Prices fell abruptly, and buyers stopped honouring their promises. Since little money had actually been paid, the main damage was to promises, not bank balances.

## What the archives show

The historian Anne Goldgar of King's College London spent years in Dutch archives for her book *Tulipmania: Money, Honor, and Knowledge in the Dutch Golden Age*. She told [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/there-never-was-real-tulip-fever-180964915/): "There weren't that many people involved and the economic repercussions were pretty minor. I couldn't find anybody that went bankrupt."

The buyers she found were mostly well-off merchants and skilled craftsmen, not chimney sweeps. The real shock was social. Contracts rested on trust, and suddenly people simply said they would not pay, while the courts declined to force them.

The wider economy barely noticed. The Dutch Republic is usually described as having had the [highest income per person in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_mania) from around 1600 to 1720, right through the supposed catastrophe.

So where did the tales of ruin come from? Goldgar points to religious pamphlets. Dutch Calvinist writers worried that sudden wealth and luxury would corrupt society, and they used the tulip trade as a moral lesson. The sweeps in the market and the ruined families: in her account, these stories come from that propaganda, later copied without checking.

## Was it even a bubble?

A bubble, in the strict sense, means people agree on prices well above what the thing is fundamentally worth. The economist Peter Garber argued that rare new flowers have always started expensive and then fallen as growers multiply them. Wikipedia summarises his finding that when hyacinths became fashionable in the early 1800s, the most expensive bulbs fell to 1 to 2 percent of their peak within 30 years. On the tulip data he collected, Garber concluded, as Smithsonian quotes him, that "the bulb speculation was not obvious madness."

Earl Thompson of UCLA went further in a [2007 paper in *Public Choice*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/pubcho/v130y2007i1p99-114.html). His argument is about the law. In late 1636, people in the trade were pushing for a rule that would let buyers walk away from contracts by paying only a small fee. On 24 February 1637, according to his account summarised on Wikipedia, the florists' guild declared that contracts written after 30 November 1636 would work that way, and Haarlem later set the exit fee at 3.5 percent of the price.

If you lose only a small fee when prices fall, but keep the full gain when they rise, the "price" in your contract is no longer a price you must pay. It is the strike price of an option. Those numbers can float far above any cash price. Thompson's abstract puts it bluntly: "There was thus nothing maniacal about prices in this period." In his view the famous prices were an artifact of contracts that had quietly turned into options.

Not everyone is convinced. Critics note that ordinary, unremarkable bulbs also rose and crashed, which Garber's rare-flower story does not explain, and some point to more money flowing through the Bank of Amsterdam at the time. The honest summary is that the price data are thin. Garber himself called the available records, gathered from different kinds of sales, "a blend of apples and oranges."

## Opinion: the lesson is about stories, not flowers

I think tulip mania still teaches something, just not what Mackay intended.

First, it shows how a good story beats a good source. A satirical pamphlet became a moralist's warning, then a respected history, then a standard example in finance books, and almost nobody went back to the contracts until the 1980s. When a market tale is too perfect, with chimney sweeps and ruined nobles, that is a reason to check it, not to repeat it.

Second, the details of a contract can matter more than the crowd's mood. If Thompson is even partly right, the most famous "mania" in history was driven in part by a change in who bore the losses. That is a very modern lesson: when someone can walk away from a bad bet cheaply, prices stop meaning what they seem to mean.

Third, the real damage was to trust. Goldgar's finding that the deepest shock was broken promises, not lost fortunes, rings true to me. Markets run on people keeping their word, and that is the part of the story worth remembering.

What would change my mind: a new archive study that finds widespread bankruptcies tied to tulip contracts in 1637, or solid price records showing cash sales, not just contracts, at the famous peak levels. Until then, I read tulip mania as a small, real market panic wrapped in a large, moralising legend.
