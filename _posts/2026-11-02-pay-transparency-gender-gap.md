For most of modern working life, what your colleague earns has been one of the best-kept secrets in the office. Many contracts even forbade talking about it. That is now changing across Europe. Since 1 November, every private employer in Greece must have a written, gender-neutral pay structure, tell job applicants the starting salary or range before the interview, and answer a worker who asks what men and women doing comparable work earn on average, according to the [Labour Ministry's circular](https://www.elinyae.gr/sites/default/files/2026-10/26923_2026.pdf) on Law 5316/2026. Italy, Slovakia, Lithuania and Malta got there first, in June.

These national laws all come from one source: the EU's [Pay Transparency Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2023/970/oj/eng), adopted in 2023. The idea behind it is simple. Unequal pay survives in silence, so break the silence. The harder question is whether that actually works, and for whom.

## What the law actually requires

The directive does several things at once, as the [official EU summary](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/equal-pay-for-equal-work-or-work-of-equal-value-between-men-and-women-rules-on-pay-transparency.html) sets out. Employers must state the starting pay or pay range in the job ad or before the interview, and may no longer ask candidates what they earned before. Clauses that stop workers from disclosing their own pay become void. Workers can ask for average pay levels, broken down by sex, for people doing the same work or work of equal value. Firms with at least 100 workers must report their gender pay gap. If a gap of 5% or more within a category of workers cannot be justified by objective, gender-neutral criteria and is not fixed within six months, the employer must carry out a joint pay assessment with workers' representatives. And in a discrimination case, the burden of proof shifts: it is the employer who must show there was no discrimination.

The starting point is a real gap. According to [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Gender_pay_gap_statistics), women's average gross hourly earnings in the EU were 11.1% below men's in 2024, a provisional figure. Worldwide the gap is larger: the [International Labour Organization](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/global-wage-growth-lowest-2008-while-women-still-earning-20-cent-less-men) put it at about 20% on average in its 2018 global estimate, and its [later guidance](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/wcmsp5/groups/public/@ed_protect/@protrav/@travail/documents/publication/wcms_849209.pdf) presents transparency as one tool to narrow it. These are raw, "unadjusted" averages. They compare all women with all men, so they mix discrimination with differences in jobs, hours and sectors. How much of the gap is discrimination is itself disputed, which is exactly why the next sections matter.

## Most of Europe missed the deadline

The deadline to turn the directive into national law was 7 June 2026. Only four countries met it in full, according to trackers compiled by [Remote Work Europe](https://remoteworkeurope.eu/insights/pay-transparency-transposition-tracker/) and the broker [Lockton](https://global.lockton.com/us/en/news-insights/eu-pay-transparency-directive-implementation-status). Greece followed in July. Germany had not even published a draft by late September; France, Spain, the Netherlands and others are aiming for 2027.

The resistance is not only bureaucratic drift. In February, the employers' federation BusinessEurope [asked the Commission](https://www.businesseurope.eu/publications/the-pay-transparency-directive-stop-the-clock-letter-from-markus-j-beyrer-to-ursula-von-der-leyen/) for a two-year "stop the clock" pause, arguing that the rules are too complex to apply. In March, [Sweden's government](https://www.regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2026/03/regeringen-vill-omforhandla-lonetransparensdirektivet/) went further and asked for the directive to be postponed and renegotiated. Its argument deserves to be taken seriously rather than waved away as a pretext. Sweden voted against the law in 2023. Its pay is set largely through collective agreements between unions and employers, and Swedish employers already have to carry out regular pay surveys. The government says the purpose of the directive is good, but that its design is so administratively burdensome that it "risks reducing the equality gains". In other words, the objection is not about whether to fight unequal pay but about who should do it: national unions and employers, or a detailed EU template.

## What happens when pay stops being secret

There is now a real body of evidence, because several countries ran this experiment before the EU did. The results are more interesting than either side's slogans.

**Denmark (2006).** Firms with more than 35 employees had to produce pay statistics by gender. A study by [Bennedsen and colleagues](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25435) found the gap fell by about 2 percentage points, roughly 13% of the original gap. But the mechanism was not that women got raises. Men's wages grew 1.7 points more slowly than in comparable firms, while women's gains were small and not statistically significant. Profits did not change, apparently because productivity also dipped.

**Britain (2018).** Firms with 250 or more employees must publish their gender pay gap on a government website. [Blundell, Duchini, Simion and Turrell](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/129500/) found this closed about 19% of the gap within affected firms. Again, the driver was slower pay growth for men, about 2.9%, through smaller bonuses and fewer male promotions. Women's pay was unaffected. The authors also found that firms more exposed to public scrutiny cut their gap the most.

**Austria (2011).** Firms had to produce pay reports by gender, but they were confidential and not published. [Gulyas, Seitz and Sinha](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpol.20210128) found no detectable effect at all on men's or women's wages or on the gap.

**Canada.** When provinces published the salaries of university staff, the gender gap among professors shrank by about 20 to 40%, according to [Baker and colleagues](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25834). Here women's pay did rise, by about one percentage point, mainly in unionised universities.

**The United States.** [Cullen and Pakzad-Hurson](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2023/05/01/Equilibrium-Effects-of-Pay-Transparency) studied state laws protecting workers' right to discuss pay. Average wages fell by about 2%. Their explanation is that once pay is visible, an employer who gives one worker a big raise knows that others will demand the same, so it refuses to give anyone that raise. Transparency weakens the bargaining power of the individual worker, which matters most for those who would otherwise have negotiated hard.

Put together, three patterns stand out. Transparency works when the information is public or at least usable, not locked in a drawer. It narrows the gap mostly by compressing pay at the top, not by lifting women. And it works better where unions or collective rules turn information into pressure.

## Beyond Europe

Europe is not alone. [Brazil](https://www.gov.br/trabalho-e-emprego/pt-br/noticias-e-conteudo/2025/novembro/mulheres-ainda-recebem-21-menos-que-homens-em-empresas-com-100-ou-mais-funcionarios) requires firms with 100 or more employees to publish pay transparency reports twice a year; the latest government report, covering more than 54,000 companies, found women earning 21.2% less than men on average. Employers' confederations challenged the law in court, citing data protection and regulatory overreach, and some firms won temporary injunctions, until the [Supreme Federal Court](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/postsnoticias/lei-que-institui-igualdade-salarial-entre-homens-e-mulheres-e-constitucional-decide-stf/) upheld it in May 2026. Japan has required employers with 301 or more employees to disclose their gender wage gap since July 2022. By January 2024, according to labour ministry figures reported by [The Asahi Shimbun](https://www.asahi.com/ajw/articles/15192092), 14,577 of 17,370 such companies had done so, and full-time women earned on average 75.2 for every 100 earned by men. The disclosures were a shock for some firms: at MUFG Bank the figure was 52.7, largely because men filled the high-paid career track and women the general one, and the bank said it would merge the two tracks. Iceland went further in 2017 with mandatory equal-pay certification; the gap fell, but a [study](https://ejournals.is/index.php/irpa/article/view/a.2022.18.2.4) found similar wage trends in certified and uncertified organisations, so the certificate itself may not have caused the fall.

## The part transparency cannot reach

The biggest limit is not legal but structural. Most of the gender gap today is not a man and a woman being paid differently for the same job in the same firm. It comes from women working in lower-paying firms and sectors, and above all from what happens after children arrive. In Denmark, [Kleven, Landais and Søgaard](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.20180010) found that children accounted for about 80% of the remaining gender earnings gap by 2013. A pay report inside a firm cannot see that. Austria's null result is partly explained this way: much of its gap lay between firms, not within them.

The directive does try to go wider than "same job, same pay", by including work of equal value. That is where the real fights will come, because comparing the value of a nurse's work with an engineer's means job evaluation, and that is exactly the part Sweden and employers call unworkable.

## Opinion: worth doing, but be honest about what it does

I think this law is worth having, and I think its supporters oversell it.

It is worth having because secrecy protects unfairness. A woman who suspects she is underpaid currently has to prove a negative with no data. The right to ask, the ban on salary-history questions, which otherwise carry yesterday's discrimination into tomorrow's contract, and the shifted burden of proof are fair tools. The evidence from Britain and Canada shows they can move numbers.

But the honest result of most studies is that the gap closes because men's pay grows more slowly, not because women's pay grows faster. Some will call that fairness: money that went to men's bonuses was never earned by being male. Others will reasonably ask whether a policy that narrows the gap while leaving women no richer, and possibly lowering average wages, as in the US study, is really a victory. Both are right about something. That is why transparency should be paired with what actually lifts women's earnings: affordable childcare, parental leave that fathers really take, and collective bargaining in the low-paid, female-dominated sectors where most women work.

Sweden's objection should also be heard. A good law that is too complex for small employers will be ignored, and a right on paper with no inspectors behind it is a right that exists only for women who can afford a lawyer. The Greek law will be judged not by its text but by whether a cleaner or a shop assistant in a ten-person firm can use it without fear.
