In Naples on 30 October, a support programme called RESPIRO presented something rare: a portrait of the children left behind when a woman is killed by her partner. It followed [124 children from 78 families](https://terredeshommes.it/comunicati/chi-sono-orfani-femminicidio-progetto-respiro/) in six regions of southern Italy. More than half were under nine when their mother died, and almost one in three was under six. In 87.7% of the documented cases, the killer was the child's own parent.

The numbers are hard to read. They also point to a question most countries have never asked out loud: when the state counts a femicide, who counts the children?

## The second victim

Femicide is not rare. The UN estimates that about [50,000 women and girls were killed by intimate partners or family members in 2024](https://www.unodc.org/unodc/en/press/releases/2025/November/137-women-and-girls-killed-every-day-by-intimate-partners-or-family-members-in-2024.html), an average of 137 a day. The same [UNODC and UN Women brief](https://www.unodc.org/documents/justice-and-prison-reform/Femicide/Femicide_Brief_2025.pdf) says Africa has the highest number of victims and the highest rate, about 3 per 100,000 women, while Europe's rate is about 0.5. Many of those women were mothers. Nobody, as far as I could find, publishes a global estimate of how many children they left.

The RESPIRO data show what these children carry. A quarter saw the killing directly, and another 32% were in the house. For 83.1%, the files record at least one earlier form of violence or serious stress, most often witnessing violence at home, psychological abuse or neglect. The killing was rarely a bolt from the blue; it was the end of a story the child had already been living.

Research agrees that the harm is wide. A [systematic review led by Eva Alisic](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26487567/) found children's outcomes ranging from post-traumatic stress and attachment problems to changes in appetite and falling school grades. Risk factors included whether the child witnessed the killing and how much conflict there was between the victim's and the perpetrator's families. But that review found only 17 studies, based on 13 samples. The honest summary is that we know these children suffer, and we know surprisingly little about what helps them most.

## Italy's law on paper

Italy is one of the few countries with a dedicated law. [Law 4 of 2018](https://www.normattiva.it/eli/stato/LEGGE/2018/01/11/4/CONSOLIDATED/20190725) covers children whose parent was killed by a spouse, civil partner or cohabiting partner. As summarised by Italy's [children's ombudsman](https://www.istat.it/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Garante_Infanzia_tutela_orfani_crimini_domestici.pdf), it gives free legal aid regardless of income, an advance payment in the criminal case of at least half the presumed damages, free psychological and medical care for as long as needed, suspension of the killer's inheritance and survivor's pension (which go to the children instead), and priority for keeping children with relatives and siblings. A state fund pays scholarships and job support.

The law is deliberately gender-neutral: it covers a father killed by a mother too. Femicide is simply the most common case.

## Half still get nothing

RESPIRO's data show the gap between the statute and the child. Services usually moved within days or weeks, in 78.5% of documented cases. But 53 of 110 children, or 48.2%, had received no clinical help before entering the programme. Among cases with information, 45.5% had not received the benefits set out in a 2020 implementing decree, and 62.8% had not received the compensation provided by a 2016 law.

The reasons recorded are mundane, which is exactly the problem: applications still pending, residence, placement or court conditions judged incompatible, bureaucratic obstacles, lack of information, and in some cases caregivers who refused the support.

These figures need care. RESPIRO is a programme sample from the south, not a national census, and children who reached it may be the ones who had fallen through other nets. Italy also lacks a register of these children. An independent observatory told a parliamentary inquiry in 2025 that it counts [more than 3,500 minors orphaned by femicide](https://www.ansa.it/sito/notizie/cronaca/2025/05/29/osservatorio-oltre-3500-i-minorenni-orfani-di-femminicidio_ee6643f8-9467-4bda-bf72-854064cbfd54.html), reconstructed from news reports. That number is plausible, but it is not an official figure and I could not confirm it.

## How other countries do it

Spain has counted these children officially since 2013. Its government data show [545 minors orphaned by partner femicide](https://violenciagenero.igualdad.gob.es/) between 2013 and today. A [2019 law](https://www.boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-2019-2975) gives them an orphan's pension even when the mother had not paid enough social security contributions. Yet Spain's attorney general said in March that families still have to [knock on "too many doors"](https://www.europapress.es/epsocial/igualdad/noticia-peramato-propone-ventanilla-unica-tramitacion-ayudas-huerfanos-violencia-genero-20260313124817.html) and called for a single office where grandparents, aunts and guardians could handle everything at once.

Argentina went further in one respect. Its 2018 [Ley Brisa](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/servicio/reparacion-economica-para-ninos-yo-adolescentes-en-el-marco-de-la-ley-27452) pays each child of a femicide victim a monthly sum equal to the minimum pension until age 21, plus health coverage. By May 2023, the government reported that [1,518 children](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/noticias/5-anos-de-la-sancion-de-la-ley-rennya-mas-de-1500-hijos-e-hijas-de-victimas-de-violencias) had received it, most aged 5 to 12. Even there, the claim passes through three agencies.

Most of the world has no such law at all. Where the UN records the highest femicide rates, data on the women themselves is still patchy, and the children are usually absorbed by relatives with no state help whatsoever.

## Why the gaps are not only indifference

It is tempting to blame cold bureaucracy, and some of it is that. But administrators face real constraints. Benefits tied to a crime often wait for a court finding, and when the killer takes his own life there is no trial. Means tests and anti-fraud rules exist because public money is involved. Courts must decide who has legal authority over the child before anyone can sign an application. Some caregivers, often grieving grandparents, refuse help because they want privacy or distrust social services, and that is their right.

Then there is the hardest question: the father. In RESPIRO's data, children went to the mother's family in 64.9% of cases and to the father's side in only 18 cases. The father's relatives may be innocent people who also love the child. Some children want contact with the surviving parent; others are terrified of him. The Alisic review counts decisions about contact with the perpetrating parent among the most drastic choices made for these children, and UK research by Women's Aid documented [19 children killed in 12 families](https://www.womensaid.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/Child-First-Nineteen-Child-Homicides-Report.pdf) in circumstances linked to contact with a violent parent. A Spanish widower who runs a scholarship fund says flatly that "an abuser is never a good father". Many family judges would not go that far, and would rather decide case by case.

## Opinion: count them, then find them

My own view is simple. These children should not have to be found by a charity. A state that records every femicide can record every child left behind, as Spain does, and can open a file for that child on the day of the killing, not after a trial that may take years or never happen.

That does not require a new philosophy, only three practical things. First, a register, so that no child depends on a news report to be counted. Second, one office and one caseworker per child, as Spain's attorney general proposes, with benefits paid provisionally and recovered later if a court rules otherwise. Third, contact decisions made from the child's view, with expert assessment, rather than from a default that a parent's rights come first.

Will money and offices heal a child who watched her mother die? No. The research suggests that a stable home and a caregiver who is not drowning matter more. But the grandmother who suddenly becomes a mother again at 65 should not also have to become a lawyer. On that point, at least, every side of this debate should agree.
