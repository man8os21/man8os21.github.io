On 28 October 2026 the European Women's Lobby, the largest umbrella group of women's organisations in the EU, published a report with one headline number: only five of the EU's 27 member states, [Cyprus, Malta, Croatia, Italy and Romania, treat femicide as a specific crime](https://womenlobby.org/press-release-the-eu-ratified-the-istanbul-convention-but-femicide-remains-silent/), and Belgium defines it in law without criminalising it. The EU's own 2024 directive on violence against women contains no definition of femicide at all.

It sounds like an obvious gap. Many lawyers, judges and governments, however, think a separate crime is unnecessary, and some think it is unfair. Both sides want fewer dead women. They disagree about what a law can actually do.

## The scale of the problem

The UN's drug and crime agency (UNODC) and UN Women estimate that [83,000 women and girls were killed intentionally in 2024](https://www.unodc.org/unodc/en/press/releases/2025/November/137-women-and-girls-killed-every-day-by-intimate-partners-or-family-members-in-2024.html), and that 60%, about 50,000, were killed by a partner or a family member. That is 137 a day, roughly one every ten minutes. For men, only 11% of homicides were committed by partners or relatives. The killing of women is concentrated at home, by people they know.

The burden is not mainly European. The UN puts the highest numbers in [Africa (about 22,600 partner or family femicides) and Asia (17,400)](https://www.unodc.org/documents/justice-and-prison-reform/Femicide/Femicide_Brief_2025.pdf), against about 2,100 in Europe, where the rate has been falling slowly since 2010. In the Americas it has stayed roughly flat.

## Three ways the law handles it

**A separate offence.** Latin America went first. According to the UN's Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLAC), [17 Latin American countries have a specific crime of femicide or feminicide](https://www.cepal.org/en/pressreleases/eclac-least-19254-femicides-have-been-registered-last-five-years-latin-america-and), from Mexico and Brazil to Chile and Bolivia. In Europe, Cyprus passed one [in July 2022](https://cyprus-mail.com/2022/07/07/femicide-made-a-distinct-crime-under-new-law), with up to life imprisonment. Croatia created the offence of ["aggravated murder of a female person" in April 2024](https://wave-network.org/femicide-criminal-offense-croatia/). Italy went furthest: its [Law 181 of December 2025](https://www.normattiva.it/eli/id/2025/12/02/25G00187/CONSOLIDATED) punishes with life imprisonment anyone who kills a woman "as an act of hatred, discrimination or domination", "of control, possession or dominion as a woman", or because she refused to start or continue a relationship. Parliament [approved it unanimously](https://apnews.com/article/italy-femicide-law-crime-gender-violence-women-99e4be4aaba9f6b940d834ed6c7cb4d0).

**An aggravating circumstance.** Here the killing stays murder, but a gender motive raises the sentence. Malta's [2022 amendment](https://parlament.mt/14th-leg/acts/act-x-of-2022/) works this way, as do Argentina, Cuba and Puerto Rico according to ECLAC. Romania is a disputed case. Its [Law 53 of April 2026](https://www.cdep.ro/ords/pls/legis/legis_pck.htp_act?an=2026&nr=53) is titled a law on "preventing and combating femicide", and the Women's Lobby counts Romania among the five. But a [Romanian criminal lawyer's analysis](https://www.andratrandafir.ro/en/legea-pentru-prevenirea-si-combaterea-femicidului-si-a-violentelor-care-il-preced/) points out that it adds no offence called femicide and no variant that applies only to female victims; it adds new aggravating elements, such as killing as a form of control within a relationship or in the context of a break-up. Whether Romania "criminalises femicide" depends on the definition you use.

**Gender-neutral murder law, plus a specialised system.** Spain is the best-known example. It has no crime called femicide, but since 2003 it has kept an official count of women killed by partners or ex-partners, and a police risk-assessment and monitoring system called VioGén. Greece also says no to a separate offence. In June 2026 Deputy Justice Minister Ioannis Bougas told parliament that [intentional homicide already carries a mandatory life sentence](https://www.iefimerida.gr/english/greece-rejects-standalone-femicide-law-government-says-life-sentence-already-mandatory-all), so a new category could not make punishment harsher, and that the constitution protects life without ranking it by gender.

## The case for naming it

Supporters make three practical arguments, not only a symbolic one.

The first is counting. If police and courts record a killing simply as "homicide", gender-motivated killings disappear into general statistics. Belgium shows this vividly. Its 2023 law defines femicide and orders police and prosecutors to collect data, with an annual report due. Yet by autumn 2026 there were still no official national figures, and the spokesperson of Belgium's equality institute explained one reason to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/recensement-des-feminicides-en-belgique-une-mission-plus-complexe-que-prevu-et-qui-continue-a-prendre-du-retard-11792348): because there is no criminal offence of femicide, "we don't have a little box we can tick". Analysts must instead cross-check victim sex, relationship and other details that different police and justice services record in different ways.

The second is investigation. A named offence forces investigators to ask about the history of control, threats and separation, which is where the warning signs usually are. ECLAC argues that criminalisation lets justice systems [investigate and prosecute "with a gender perspective"](https://www.cepal.org/en/pressreleases/eclac-least-19254-femicides-have-been-registered-last-five-years-latin-america-and).

The third is recognition. The European Parliament's research service notes that [widely publicised killings have driven reforms](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EPRS_BRI(2026)789304) in several member states, and that the Parliament has asked for EU-level recognition to improve legal clarity and comparable data.

## The case against

The critics are not only people who dislike feminism. Much of the strongest criticism comes from criminal lawyers who share the goal.

In Italy, legal scholars argued that words like "hatred", "domination" and "as a woman" are [too vague to prove beyond reasonable doubt](https://www.ordineavvocatitorino.it/sites/default/files/relazione%20FEMMINICIDIO%20AVV.%20CAMPAGNA.pdf), and that they push courts towards judging a defendant's inner attitudes rather than his acts. One submission to the Italian parliament pointed out that killing a spouse or partner [could already be punished with life imprisonment](https://documenti.camera.it/leg19/documentiAcquisiti/COM02/Audizioni/leg19.com02.Audizioni.Memoria.PUBBLICO.ideGes.72207.14-10-2025-10-35-53.212.pdf), and doubted deterrence: it noted that about a third of the men who commit these killings kill themselves or try to afterwards, which is not the profile of someone weighing sentences. In Croatia, women's groups themselves reported that [Supreme Court judges and constitutional experts criticised the new offence](https://wave-network.org/femicide-criminal-offense-croatia/) as discrimination against men.

That equality argument deserves a fair hearing. Men are also killed by partners and relatives, even if far less often. A law that protects only one sex must justify the difference, and in most constitutions the burden is on the state.

## What the evidence says

The honest answer is that a separate offence, by itself, has no proven effect on the number of killings.

Mexico was a pioneer, so it is the best test. A study by economist Roxana Gutiérrez-Romero used the different dates on which Mexican states adopted femicide laws and found that [the laws had no measurable impact](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06722) on femicides, homicides, disappearances or suicides of women, whatever the length of the prison sentence. Her conclusion is that laws are insufficient "in contexts of impunity".

India offers a much older example. It has long had a gender-specific offence, "dowry death", now [Section 80 of the new criminal code](https://theprint.in/judiciary/20-dowry-deaths-a-day-but-conviction-for-1-in-6-a-tale-of-evidentiary-hurdles-delayed-trials-apathy/2738252/), with at least seven years in prison. Yet official data cited by ThePrint record 6,450 dowry deaths in 2022, with state conviction rates of about 11–17%, which the reporting blames on police indifference, poor evidence, hostile witnesses and trials that drag on for years. A name in the code did not fix the institutions around it.

Spain points the other way. Without a femicide offence, the official count of women killed by partners or ex-partners [fell from 71 in 2003 to 50 in 2022](https://www.interior.gob.es/opencms/es/detalle/articulo/Los-asesinatos-por-violencia-de-genero-en-Espana-han-descendido-un-29-en-los-ultimos-20-anos), and stood at [49 in 2024](https://violenciagenero.igualdad.gob.es/wp-content/uploads/Principales_datos_BEA_2024.pdf). That is still a woman almost every week, and Spain cannot prove which of its measures caused the fall, but its record shows that risk assessment, monitoring and an official count can exist without a new crime.

Turkey shows the political limits of the debate. In 2021 it [left the Istanbul Convention](https://apnews.com/article/middle-east-europe-turkey-e140886dba6cc70d38ad49e148758796), the Council of Europe treaty on violence against women. Its government said the treaty had been used to [promote values it considers incompatible with Turkish family values](https://www.iletisim.gov.tr/turkce/haberler/detay/statement-regarding-turkeys-withdrawal-from-the-istanbul-convention) and that it would protect women through its own laws. For a large part of the world, the argument is not about the wording of a crime but about whether gender-based violence is a category the state should use at all.

## Opinion: name it, but don't stop there

*This section is my opinion.*

I think the critics are right about one thing and wrong about another. They are right that a life sentence does not stop a man who has already decided to kill the woman who left him. Italy and Greece already punished such killings with life imprisonment before any new label. A new label will not make that sentence frighten him more.

They are wrong to treat naming as merely symbolic. Belgium shows that what the law does not name, the state struggles to count, and what it does not count, it cannot test. Whether the right tool is a separate offence or an aggravating circumstance is a reasonable legal argument. Refusing any legal recognition, as Greece does, makes it harder to know whether anything is working.

But the women who survive are saved earlier, long before any courtroom: when a police officer takes a threat seriously, when a risk assessment flags the separation, when a protection order is actually enforced. Mexico and India show that a named crime without those things changes very little. If Europe adopts the word, it should be judged by one test only: fewer women killed by the men who claimed to love them.
